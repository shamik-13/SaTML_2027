\begin{abstract}
Statistical trust layers can combine conformal evidence, grouped security hypotheses, and online
false-discovery-rate control to turn ML detector scores into calibrated alerts. We show that this
composition creates two system-level failure modes and characterize their security consequences.

First, we derive an ex-ante feasibility law for bounded conformal evidence under two families of
arrival-time controllers. It yields a finite discovery horizon beyond which a rejection-free run cannot
alert regardless of detector score, and an optimal calibration-to-horizon scaling; at $k=1$, LOND/e-LOND
require $20$ calibration units per tested hypothesis even under the best cold-start allocation.

Second, we formalize adversarial robustness when an attacker can enlarge its own grouped hypothesis and
prove a class-wide impossibility: no alert-capable symmetric e-merging family is robust to arbitrary
zero-evidence padding. For the arithmetic mean, we derive the exact suppression cost.

Finally, we validate these effects on LSPR23 and AIT-LDSv2.0. Real-flow replay confirms padding
suppression, while joint controller-state reruns show that suppressing early alerts can reduce campaign
cost by roughly two orders of magnitude relative to independently pricing alerts.

These results show that statistical validity of individual components does not guarantee feasibility or
adversarial robustness of the composed alerting system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A network intrusion detector produces scores; a security operations centre (SOC) acts on alerts. AUROC, AUPRC, and threshold recall describe a classifier, but they do not tell an
operator how much of the resulting alert queue is false. At the low base rates typical of intrusion
detection, even a strong classifier can produce a queue dominated by false
positives~\cite{axelsson1999baserate,axelsson2000baserate}.

Online multiple testing provides a natural control layer. At each time $t$, a procedure tests one
hypothesis, makes an immediate decision, and updates future testing levels while controlling an online
false-discovery criterion~\cite{javanmard2015lond,ramdas2017lordpp,xu2024elond}. Conformal calibration
can turn detector scores into distribution-free evidence under
exchangeability~\cite{vovk2005alrw,bates2023outliers}; e-values can support false-discovery-rate (FDR) control under
arbitrary dependence~\cite{vovk2021evalues,wang2022evaluebh}. These components form a modular
\emph{statistical trust layer}: detector $\to$ conformal evidence $\to$ security grouping $\to$
online controller $\to$ alert (\cref{fig:system}). Prior work combines subsets of this chain: anomaly
scores with online FDR control, conformal evidence with online or batch decisions, or calibrated
streaming intrusion alerts~\cite{rebjock2021online,farzaneh2025coad,wang2025multilayer,caliburn2026,gao2026boundary}.
We assemble the full composition as an evaluated design and study a failure mode that is absent from the guarantee of any
single module.

The first problem is feasibility. A finite calibration set places a hard upper bound on deterministic
conformal evidence, while an uninterrupted online controller distributes a finite error budget over a
potentially long stream. Along a rejection-free run, its offered levels can eventually become too
small for even the maximum admissible evidence. At that point, no detector output can trigger an
alert. This structural inability to report a discovery and its onset
can be calculated before deployment.

A natural repair is to test fewer hypotheses. SOCs already aggregate flows into host-, service-, or
time-based episodes; coarser grouping reduces the horizon that the controller must cover. Yet the same operation determines which observations share a hypothesis. When group membership depends on endpoints, services, and time, an attacker can often directly influence it by generating additional traffic.

We connect these facts into one causal chain. Our contributions are:

\emph{A deployment-level feasibility law (\cref{sec:feasibility}).} We turn the interaction
between finite conformal resolution and online error spending into an attained, ex-ante deployment
criterion: given $\nCal$, $k$, and $T$, one can decide before deployment whether a covered controller
can possibly report an alert throughout the intended horizon, independently of the detector. Grouping restores
feasibility at exactly the criterion's exchange rate. Switching the
primary window from a host-pair to a source-host key raises canonical detections from $3$ to $46$ and the
median exact suppression cost, from $24$ to $496$ added flows. So one grouping
choice sets feasibility, alert semantics, and attack cost together (\cref{sec:granularity}).

\emph{A cross-arity security impossibility (\cref{sec:attack}).} E-merging theory studies a fixed set of
e-values; our adversary can enlarge its own hypothesis. We formalise robustness across arities and
prove that, under attacker-controlled, unbounded membership, no symmetric e-merging family can both
attain an alert threshold and remain above it after arbitrary zero-evidence padding. For the arithmetic mean, the bound is tight and gives the exact
suppression cost.

\emph{An operational attack boundary (\cref{sec:attack,sec:fixes}).} We evaluate the attack along a
capability ladder: a sequential oracle that sizes each pad exactly from the realised controller state, a
retrospective attacker that replays real benign-to-victim traffic, and a temporally restricted,
state-free attacker limited to traffic the victim had already received and to its own episode size. The distance between
the rungs is a measured result rather than an assumption: on AIT-LDSv2.0 the retrospective attacker
suppresses 78 of 79 flow-only true detections under the canonical order, while the victim-prior and
state-free arms leave 24 to 65 standing, because template availability and attacker knowledge bind before
the algebra does. Controller
allocation raises the price of an individual alert by two orders of magnitude, but a joint rerun on
LSPR23 shows a controller-state feedback: once early alerts are suppressed, later alerts remain near
their cheaper cold-start levels. A capacity-constrained attacker shows where a membership bound would
interrupt the attack; we do not present that constraint as an end-to-end admission policy.

The key distinction is between weak evidence and an impossible decision. Once evidence cannot exceed
$\ceil$, level decay produces a calculable step after which no admissible detector output can trigger
a discovery. Maximising that horizon over spending sequences yields an attained deployment boundary in
$\nCal$, $k$, and $T$ (\cref{cor:budget}), rather than another post-hoc measure of detection power.

Our main evaluation uses 16,353,511 flows from LSPR23~\cite{lspr23}. The chronological position 0.55
defines the primary window, and 0.62 provides a secondary check. Position 0.85 has a benign tail that
violates marginal e-validity, so we use it only as an explicitly marked stress window for mechanism and
cost measurements. AIT-LDSv2.0 provides a second, benign-inclusive setting. The appendices report the
full windows, seeds, ordering variants, and validity diagnostics; \cref{fig:system} maps the argument
to the paper structure.

\section{Statistical Trust Layer and Evaluation Model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Bounded evidence and online decisions}\label{sec:controllers}

Flows $x_1,x_2,\ldots$ arrive in timestamp order, and a detector $f$ assigns each flow a score
$s_i=f(x_i)$. We convert the score to threshold conformal evidence using a benign calibration set
$\Cal$ that is disjoint from training and precedes deployment. Let
$K_i=1+\#\{c\in\Cal:s_c\ge s_i\}$ denote the rank of $s_i$ from the top of the calibration scores,
with ties resolved against the test flow. At rank depth $1\le k\le\nCal+1$, the flow e-value is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evalue}
e_i=\frac{\nCal+1}{k}\,\mathbf 1\{K_i\le k\},
\end{equation}
with $\mathbb E[e_i]\le1$ under the null and the relevant exchangeability premise, and
$p_i=\min(1,1/e_i)$. The construction is two-point: a flow either reaches the calibration tail or
receives zero. It therefore imposes the hard ceiling
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ceiling}
\ceil=\frac{\nCal+1}{k}.
\end{equation}
No detector score can produce evidence above $\ceil$. This ceiling follows from the finite resolution
of deterministic rank-based conformal evidence~\cite{vovk2005alrw,hennhofer2026resolution}; it does
not depend on the detector. Randomised smoothing removes the corresponding floor, and
\cref{app:taxonomy} evaluates that alternative.

We use $\alpha$ for the target error level and $\alphat$ for the level offered at step $t$; in
particular, $\alpha_T$ denotes the offered level at step $T$, not a product. The procedure rejects
hypothesis $t$ when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reject}
\Ev(G_t)\ \ge\ 1/\alphat .
\end{equation}
Together, \eqref{eq:ceiling} and \eqref{eq:reject} give the paper's central feasibility condition: a
rejection at step $t$ requires $\alphat\ge1/\ceil$, regardless of the detector score. We use e-LOND as
the principal controller because it controls $\FDR$ under arbitrary dependence when the group
e-values are marginally valid~\cite{xu2024elond}. We instantiate the horizon results for LOND,
e-LOND, e-LORD, and LORD++; \cref{app:feasibility} covers additional procedures and escape mechanisms.
All experiments use $q=\alpha=0.05$, $w_0=0.025$, and $k=1$.

We compare two spending regimes. Let $(\gamma_j)_{j\ge1}$ be the spending sequence. The
\emph{horizon-free} regime uses $\gamma_j=j^{-1.6}/\zeta(1.6)$, abbreviated
$\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$, and represents uninterrupted operation without a known terminal horizon.
The \emph{horizon-aware} regime uses the horizon-uniform allocation
$\gamma_j=T^{-1}\mathbf 1\{j\le T\}$, abbreviated $\gamma_j=1/T$. It assumes a known stream length and
maximises the smallest cold-start level over $1,\ldots,T$, making it the strongest cold-start
benchmark covered by \cref{cor:budget}. This comparison separates the merger's structural exposure
from the controller's choice of allocation.

\subsection{Grouped security hypotheses}\label{sec:grouping}

Because SOC investigations rarely operate at flow granularity, we group flows into episodes $G_j$
using pre-committed metadata and a time bucket. Our main unit is a source--destination host pair within
a two-hour bucket. The controller receives the arithmetic mean
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean}
\Ev(G_j)=\frac1{m_j}\sum_{i\in G_j}e_i,\qquad m_j=\lvert G_j\rvert .
\end{equation}
At every fixed arity, the mean is a valid e-merging rule under arbitrary dependence. Unlike the
maximum, it does not systematically increase with group size, so it is the symmetric rule we evaluate.

\emph{Guarantee scope.} The group-level premise is stated as \cref{assump:groupval}. Neither the
discovery-horizon results nor \cref{thm:padding} requires it; it is needed only to read the empirical
e-LOND runs as nominally $\FDR$-controlled. The nominal
$\FDR$ guarantee for an empirical e-LOND run requires more than
unconditional flow-level validity; what it needs is a marginally valid e-value for every true-null
group. The premise we adopt is one sufficient condition for that, not a necessary one: for each
true-null group $G_j$, every benign member remains e-valid conditional on the pre-committed local
metadata that fixes the key, membership, arity, order position, and horizon; writing $\mathcal M_j$ for
the $\sigma$-field that metadata generates, $\mathbb E[e_i\mid\mathcal M_j]\le1$ for every benign
$i\in G_j$. Endpoint and service metadata can correlate with the features driving the score, so the
unconditional conformal statement does not imply this conditional one. Under this premise, linearity
gives marginally valid group e-values even with random arity and arbitrary dependence within and
between groups; other constructions of valid group e-values are possible, and \cref{app:groupval}
gives one in which the premise holds by construction. \Cref{app:validity,tab:deps} audit and track its use.

\subsection{Threat model}\label{sec:threat}

We assume that the defender correctly implements the detector, calibration, grouping, aggregation,
and controller. The adversary controls traffic from an attacker-operated or compromised host and can
open ordinary connections to the destination it is already attacking before the same bucket closes.
Under the key $(\mathrm{SrcIP},\mathrm{DstIP},\text{bucket})$, those connections join the attacker's
tested hypothesis. The adversary neither changes the detector or calibration code nor modifies stored
scores or malicious flows; its goal is to suppress the alert on its own grouped activity.

We test, rather than assume, that suitable ordinary traffic contributes zero evidence.
\Cref{sec:replay} uses real LSPR23 flows chosen before deployment by network-observable service
frequency alone. \Cref{sec:transferattack} repeats the attack on AIT with genuine benign traffic sent
to attacked victims, then separates a retrospective deployment-wide template pool from traffic that
had reached the victim before the attacker's last flow in the target episode. The latter restricts
temporal availability, but it
does not by itself make every template observable to a compromised source: such a host need not see
other benign traffic received by the victim. We therefore report these pools as attacker-capability
boundaries, not as demonstrated online attacks.

We distinguish two knowledge models. The \textbf{exact attack cost} $r^{\star}_t$ uses the realised
group evidence and live controller level, and is therefore an oracle lower bound. \textbf{State-free
over-provisioning} instead uses a fixed multiplier of the attacker's own episode size
(\cref{sec:paddingcost}).

Groups that close in the same bucket still require an order. Our main results use a \textbf{canonical}
order: a deterministic hash of each group's metadata key, with collisions broken by the key itself.
Because it ignores arrival time, appending flows to one group does not move any other hypothesis; this
preserves the group-validity premise on the attacked stream. Canonicalisation is not a security
mechanism (\cref{sec:transfer}). We also audit first-flow arrival order, which exposes an attacker
timing lever and gives a detector-favourable upper bound on LSPR23 detections. Every such contrast is
labelled explicitly.

\subsection{Data and chronological protocol}\label{sec:data}

LSPR23~\cite{lspr23} contains 16,353,511 flows from the 161.5-hour Locked Shields live-fire exercise;
1,644,599 flows are malicious (10.06\%, or $0.48$--$0.81\%$ of episodes). We sort the data by timestamp
and construct chronological training, benign calibration, and deployment blocks, with calibration and
test each occupying 15\% of $N$. No data are randomly mixed across time.

The five deployment positions are overlapping chronological views of one exercise, not five datasets
and not independent replications. Adjacent windows share $46.7$--$53.3\%$ of their deployment flows;
each deployment block coincides, to within one flow, with the calibration block of the position $0.15$
later; and the training pools $[0,i_1)$ are nested. We therefore report each window separately and use
position 0.62 as a robustness check rather than confirmatory replication. \Cref{app:splits} gives the
exact geometry. Two detector seeds change the scores but not any feasibility quantity, and we never
pool them.

The primary detector is HistGradientBoosting over the protocol and 32 flow timing and volume features.
Using a standard classifier keeps the analysis focused on the trust layer. A second detector adds six
strictly causal host-context features (\cref{sec:transferattack}). AIT-LDSv2.0~\cite{aitlds} provides
an external, benign-inclusive transfer setting in which attacked hosts also receive ordinary traffic.
\Cref{app:procedures} gives the features, preprocessing, and implementation checks.

We fixed positions 0.55 (primary) and 0.62 (secondary) before running any validity diagnostic. At
$k=1$, only $0$--$3$ benign deployment flows reach the calibration tail in each of the four non-stress
windows, too few to establish validity. At position 0.85, 46 benign flows fire---$50.9\times$ the
nominal rate---which refutes marginal e-validity ($\mathbb E[e]\approx51$). We therefore use 0.85 only
as a \emph{known-invalid} stress window for algebraic, mechanism, and cost results that do not require
valid evidence, and label every use accordingly.

Unless stated otherwise, measured results use detector seed 0, canonical within-bucket order, and the
horizon-free regime at the named window. \Cref{app:conventions} gives the complete reporting
conventions.

\section{Bounded Evidence Creates a Finite Discovery Horizon}\label{sec:feasibility}

Detection power asks whether malicious activity produces strong evidence. \emph{Feasibility} asks a
more basic question: could the controller accept even the strongest admissible evidence? Combining
\eqref{eq:reject} and \eqref{eq:ceiling} gives the necessary condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:feas}
\alphat\ \ge\ 1/\ceil ,
\end{equation}
which is determined by $\nCal$, $k$, and the offered level before detector performance enters. We call
step $t$ \textbf{evidence-feasible} when \eqref{eq:feas} holds. On a rejection-free run, the final such
step is the \textbf{discovery horizon} $t^{\star}$. Beyond it, the run is \textbf{absorbed}: even the
maximum admissible evidence cannot produce a rejection. We call this operational state
\textbf{structural silence}. A procedure is \textbf{cold-start feasible through $T$} if every
$t\le T$ remains evidence-feasible before the first rejection.

Weak detectors, rare attacks, and unlucky orderings can all reduce recall. Structural silence is
different: $\nCal$, $k$, and $T$ determine it independently of the observed scores. Accordingly, the
results in this section concern the rejection rule itself and require neither valid e-values nor
\cref{assump:groupval}; those conditions matter only for the nominal $\FDR$ interpretation
(\cref{tab:deps}).

\subsection{One headline result, two controller forms}\label{sec:horizon}

\begin{theorem}[Finite discovery horizon, multiplicative family]\label{thm:family1}
Let the evidence be bounded by $\ceil<\infty$ and the controller run without restart, with
$\alphat=\alpha\,\gamma_t\,g(\mathcal H_{t-1})$, $\gamma\ge0$, $\sum_t\gamma_t\le1$ and
$g\le c_g(R_{t-1}+1)^d$ for the rejection count $R$ and some $d\ge0$ (which the step below uses; every
procedure we cover has $d\in\{0,1\}$). Since $R_{t-1}\le t-1$, a rejection is possible at
$t$ only if $\gamma_t t^d\ge1/(\alpha c_g\ceil)$. If $\gamma_t t^d\to0$, only finitely many steps are
feasible on a rejection-free run; if that set is non-empty it has a last element $t^{\star}$ and the run
is absorbed from $t^{\star}+1$ onward, and if it is empty the run is absorbed from step $1$. No monotonicity of $\gamma$ is needed: a set of steps that is
finite already has a maximum.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Finite discovery horizon, lag-sum family]\label{thm:family2}
Let the evidence be bounded by $\ceil<\infty$ and
$\alphat=w_0\gamma_t+\sum_j a_j\gamma_{t-\tau_j}$ over prior rejection times $\tau_j<t$, with
$w_0\ge0$, $0\le a_j\le\alpha$, $\gamma\ge0$ and $\sum_t\gamma_t<\infty$. During a rejection-free run of length $\Delta$ every lag
is distinct and at least $\Delta$, so $\alphat\le(w_0+\alpha)\sum_{u\ge\Delta}\gamma_u$. The tail sum
tends to zero, so $\Delta^{\star}=\min\{\Delta:(w_0+\alpha)\sum_{u\ge\Delta}\gamma_u<1/\ceil\}$ is finite
and the run is absorbed once $\Delta\ge\Delta^{\star}$, with no monotonicity assumption on $\gamma$.
\end{theorem}

The two theorems capture the same mechanism for the two common forms of arrival-time levels, but their
scope differs. \Cref{thm:family1} uses $R_{t-1}\le t-1$ to give a bound uniform over the preceding
rejection history. \Cref{thm:family2} is a rejection-gap result: its tail bound applies after a gap of
length $\Delta$, and a new rejection starts a new gap. Along the relevant rejection-free prefix, the
offered level eventually falls below the fixed floor $1/\ceil$. Once that holds, no subsequent
observation can trigger a rejection, because every group's evidence is bounded by $\ceil$, and since
only a rejection would raise the level, the state is absorbing unless the mechanism itself changes.
\Cref{thm:family1} covers LOND, e-LOND, and e-LORD, while \cref{thm:family2} covers LORD++.
The sequence $\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$ satisfies both sets of conditions. Full proofs and the precise
hypothesis scope appear in \cref{app:proofs}.

Huo et al.~\cite{huo2024realtime} identify the conformal floor $1/(\nCal+1)$ and observe poor
online-testing behaviour for LOND, SAFFRON, and ADDIS. Our result characterises when this interaction
becomes structural: for the two controller forms above, only finitely many steps are feasible and a
sufficiently long rejection-free run becomes permanently silent. The result applies to both p-value
and e-value procedures and identifies the relevant procedure--sequence pairs. The calibration law
below then converts this mechanism into an ex-ante deployment test.

\subsection{The best-case calibration--horizon exchange rate}\label{sec:exchange}

Before the first rejection, a procedure can spend only its cold-start budget. Let $c_0$ denote the
coefficient multiplying the spending weight: $c_0=w_0$ for a level-$w_0$ procedure such as LORD++, and
$c_0=\alpha=2w_0$ for LOND and e-LOND. Among non-negative sequences with total mass at most one, the
horizon-uniform allocation $\gamma_t=T^{-1}\mathbf 1\{t\le T\}$ maximises
$\min_{t\le T}\gamma_t$, and therefore maximises the cold-start horizon. \Cref{app:proofs} gives the
proof.

\begin{corollary}[Horizon-uniform feasibility]\label{cor:budget}
Cold-start feasibility over a horizon $T$ --- every step $t\le T$ evidence-feasible before the first
rejection, which the ceiling decides and detection power does not --- requires $\nCal\ge k/\alpha_T-1$,
for $\alpha_T$ the level offered at step $T$. Under the horizon-uniform allocation
$\gamma_t=T^{-1}\mathbf 1\{t\le T\}$, where $\alpha_T$ is also the smallest level offered over the
horizon, the condition is necessary \emph{and} sufficient and reads
\[
\nCal\ \ge\ \frac{kT}{c_0}-1 .
\]
The requirement is a scale-free count ratio $k/c_0$: at $k=1$ it is $20$ calibration units per
hypothesis for LOND/e-LOND and $40$ for LORD++, whatever the unit is --- exactly $20T-1$ and $40T-1$
over a horizon $T$, the ratio being the rate rather than the count.
\end{corollary}

\Cref{cor:budget} is the best cold-start scaling attainable by any pre-committed allocation. It turns
the evidence ceiling into a sizing rule: the count ratio $k/c_0$ is independent of both the dataset and
the alerting unit. Reducing the number of tested hypotheses therefore reduces the required calibration
size in direct proportion, which motivates the grouping analysis in \cref{sec:granularity}. The same
invariance does not hold in calendar time because calibration and deployment produce units at
different rates (\cref{app:feasibility}). Kr\"onert et al.~\cite{kronert2024fdr} derive an algebraically
related equality for a windowed procedure; \cref{sec:related} distinguishes the two settings.

We summarise feasibility by the allocation-optimal, horizon-uniform ratio
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho}
\rho_p=\frac{\ceil\,c_{0,p}}{T}=\frac{(\nCal+1)\,c_{0,p}}{kT}.
\end{equation}
For procedure $p$ under horizon-uniform allocation, $\rho_p\ge1$ is necessary and sufficient for
cold-start feasibility through $T$ by \cref{cor:budget}. Under another pre-committed allocation
$\gamma$, the corresponding minimum-level ratio is
$\rho_p(\gamma)=\ceil\,c_{0,p}\min_{t\le T}\gamma_t\le\rho_p$. Throughout the paper, $\rho$ denotes the
horizon-uniform ratio, with feasibility at $1$.

At network scale, the requirement is substantial (\cref{fig:envelope}). Testing all 16,353,511 flows
in one uninterrupted run requires about $3.3\times10^{8}$ calibration flows for LOND/e-LOND and
$6.5\times10^{8}$ for LORD++. The smaller value is already 20 times the entire exercise, compared with
the $1.8$--$2.4$ million benign calibration flows available before each deployment window. Scaling
calibration and deployment together does not close the gap because the ratio $k/c_0$ remains fixed.
Moreover, these figures assume the cheapest allocation: under horizon-free
$\gamma\propto j^{-1.6}$, the same horizon requires about $1.6\times10^{13}$ calibration flows for
LOND/e-LOND and $3.2\times10^{13}$ for LORD++.

\subsection{Feasibility is not detection power}\label{sec:feasnotdet}

Feasibility is necessary for detection, but it does not guarantee detection. Two-hour host-pair
episodes reduce $T$ to 31,568--57,368, making horizon-uniform e-LOND feasible in all five windows
($\rho=2.13$--$3.34$). Under horizon-free spending, however, useful level remains concentrated in a
short prefix: before the first discovery, only the first 748--902 hypotheses can reject, or
$1.6$--$2.6\%$ of the stream. A near-ceiling malicious episode must land inside that prefix. Under the
canonical order, e-LOND therefore detects three of 275 malicious episodes in the primary window and
eleven in the secondary window (\cref{tab:costcurve}).

The evidence ceiling and the spending sequence play different roles. At flow granularity, the
shortfall is allocation-optimal, so reallocation cannot remove it. At the grouped unit, allocation
matters: horizon-aware uniform spending is feasible ($\rho=2.13$) and detects $105$ of 275 malicious
episodes at the primary window with no false discoveries, versus three detections under
$\gamma\propto j^{-1.6}$ (\cref{tab:costcurve}). Two additional configurations show the same
separation (\cref{app:feasnotpower}). Horizon-free spending exposes the short feasible prefix of an
anytime deployment; horizon-aware spending or restart restores substantial power. Under first-flow
order on both legs at the primary window and seed 0, e-LORD with horizon-aware weighting reaches
$101$ detections with no false discoveries, and a two-hour periodic restart that keeps the
deployment-wide budget takes e-LOND from $18$ to $55$ rejections (\cref{app:taxonomy}). We price the
padding attack under both regimes in \cref{sec:costcurve}.

Across the five canonical-order positions, e-LOND detects $3$, $11$, $0$, $0$, and $34$ episodes. The
two zero counts arise because no episode clears its offered level, not because the controller is
absorbed. The 34 detections occur in the known-invalid stress window.

A detector with better ranking can even produce fewer alerts. Adding six strictly causal host-context
features raises primary-window AUROC from 0.916 to 0.954 but reduces e-LOND detections from 18 to two
under first-flow order across both legs. Because $\nCal$, $k$, and $T$ are unchanged, so is $\rho$:
ranking quality affects power but cannot move the feasibility boundary. \Cref{app:feasibility} reports
the full matrices, an Isolation Forest comparison, and a low-prevalence analysis in which the
probability that e-LOND ever rejects falls to $0$--$40\%$ while $\rho$ remains fixed.

\subsection{Procedures outside the theorems}\label{sec:escapes}

The horizon theorems cover arrival-time controllers whose effective spending index advances with
elapsed hypotheses or rejection lags. ADDIS can avoid elapsed-time decay by selectively advancing its
index. History-wide procedures such as online e-BH also fall outside the predicate because they
reconsider earlier hypotheses instead of fixing a decision level at arrival. These alternatives move
the constraint rather than making evidence scale irrelevant. \Cref{app:feasibility} provides the full
taxonomy and measurements, while \cref{app:state} shows how an evidence-conditioned index creates a
separate attacker-controlled state variable.

\section{Grouping Restores Feasibility by Changing the Security Unit}\label{sec:granularity}

\Cref{cor:budget} makes the alerting unit a design variable. When the calibration corpus is fixed,
feasibility can be restored by reducing $T$: test groups of flows instead of individual flows.
Replacing flow-level hypotheses with episodes changes the horizon from $T_{\mathrm{flow}}$ to
$T_{\mathrm{group}}$ and gives the allocation-optimal requirement---the minimum calibration size over
pre-committed spending allocations---
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exchange}
\nCal_{\min}+1=\frac{kT_{\mathrm{group}}}{c_0},
\end{equation}
Thus $\nCal+1$ falls in direct proportion to the number of tested hypotheses. For the covered
procedures under uninterrupted control, grouping restores cold-start feasibility at this exact
exchange rate.

Grouping changes more than $T$. Moving from a host-pair key to a source-host key raises the primary
window's canonical detections from $3$ to $46$ under horizon-free spending, while the median exact
suppression cost---an oracle lower bound---rises from $24$ to $496$ added flows. The grouping
key therefore changes feasibility, alert semantics, and attack cost together. It becomes a membership
defence only when the attacker cannot cheaply co-occupy it; our operational keys include an endpoint
that the attacker controls.

We evaluate five SOC-relevant grouping families over six bucket widths and a whole-deployment bucket,
for 35 configurations per window and seed (\cref{app:granularity}). Every family follows the predicted
linear relationship between hypothesis count and required calibration, and each becomes feasible once
the bucket is sufficiently coarse. Source-host grouping is feasible at every bucket, reducing each
window to $763$--$24{,}043$ episodes. \Cref{apptab:grouping} reports the primary window at seed 0;
feasibility itself depends only on $T$.

The cost is lower semantic resolution. Episode recall cannot be compared directly across bucket
widths because both the positive unit and its count change. We therefore fix an atomic reference:
every malicious source--destination episode in a five-minute bucket is one atom, independent of the
controller's alerting bucket. For an issued alert, \textbf{alert blur} is the number of distinct
malicious atoms that it represents.

We measure blur under first-flow order, which produces alerts at every width and holds ordering fixed
as granularity changes. In the primary window, mean blur grows from $1.0$ at five minutes to $7.2$ at
one hour, $16.1$ at the two-hour headline unit, and $38.5$ at one day
(\cref{fig:granularity}). Fixed atomic coverage increases at the same time, but each alert identifies
a progressively broader span of malicious activity.

The same operation that restores feasibility decides which flows share a hypothesis. When endpoints,
services, or timing determine membership, the attacker can influence the composition of the alerting
unit directly.

\section{Padding Evasion through Attacker-Influenceable Membership}\label{sec:attack}

\subsection{Padding the tested hypothesis}


An attack episode is a source--destination pair within a two-hour bucket. Before that bucket closes,
the attacker opens additional ordinary connections to the same destination, placing them in the same
hypothesis. The malicious flows and all existing detector scores remain unchanged. Under the two-point
evidence construction, each added flow that misses the calibration tail contributes exactly zero, so
$r$ additions change the aggregate from $F_m(e_1,\ldots,e_m)$ to
$F_{m+r}(e_1,\ldots,e_m,0^r)$.

The following theorem shows that, under attacker-controlled, unbounded membership, no alert-capable
symmetric e-merging family can ignore arbitrary padding while preserving validity. Like the feasibility results, suppression of a specified alert is
a property of the aggregation rule and realised controller level; it does not require valid e-values
or \cref{assump:groupval} (\cref{tab:deps}). Nor does validity prevent it: \cref{app:groupval} gives
a four-hypothesis construction in which every true-null e-value is exactly valid by exchangeability
and one appended zero-evidence flow still erases the only alert.

\subsection{Symmetric alerting and arbitrary padding invariance are incompatible}\label{sec:impossibility}


\begin{definition}[$\theta$-padding-robustness, attainment]\label{def:padding}
A family $\{F_N\}_{N\ge1}$ of e-merging functions is $\theta$-padding-robust, for $\theta>1$, if for every
$m\ge1$, every finite $x\in[0,\infty)^m$ with $F_m(x)\ge\theta$ and every integer $r\ge0$,
$F_{m+r}(x,0^{r})\ge\theta$. It
\emph{attains} $\theta$ if some finite input has $F_m(x)\ge\theta$.
\end{definition}

Attainment excludes rules that can never fire, such as $F\equiv1$ (\cref{app:attain}).

\begin{theorem}[Symmetric padding impossibility]\label{thm:padding}
No family of symmetric e-merging functions that attains $\theta$ is $\theta$-padding-robust, for any
$\theta>1$. Quantitatively, for every symmetric e-merging family, every $m\ge1$, every finite
$x\in[0,\infty)^m$, every $\theta>1$ and every integer $r\ge0$,
\[
F_{m+r}(x,0^{r})<\theta\qquad\text{whenever}\qquad r>\frac{\sum_i x_i}{\theta}-m .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Vovk and Wang~\cite[Prop.~3.1]{vovk2021evalues} show that the arithmetic mean \emph{essentially}
dominates every symmetric e-merging function: $F(e)>1$ implies $\mathrm{mean}(e)\ge F(e)$. A case split therefore gives
$F_N(x)\le\max\{1,\mathrm{mean}(x)\}$ on finite inputs. Padding gives
$\mathrm{mean}(x,0^{r})=\sum_ix_i/(m+r)$, which falls below $\theta$ once $r>\sum_ix_i/\theta-m$, and
attainment supplies an $x$ to which this applies. The mean meets the bound with equality, so the
quantitative statement is tight. \Cref{app:proofs} gives the full proof, the scaled-rule edge case, and
an independent negatively-dependent special case.
\end{proof}

Vovk and Wang's domination result~\cite[Prop.~3.1]{vovk2021evalues} compares e-merging rules at a fixed
arity: it asks how best to combine a given set of e-values. Our adversary changes that set.
\Cref{def:padding} captures the required cross-arity property, and \cref{thm:padding} establishes an
exact incompatibility under attacker-controlled, unbounded membership: a symmetric e-merging family can
withstand arbitrary zero-evidence additions or attain an alert threshold, but not both. Whenever a symmetric merger issues an alert, the attainment
condition holds at that threshold (\cref{app:attain}). The arithmetic mean reaches the quantitative
bound, yielding the exact cost in \eqref{eq:rstar}. Substituting another symmetric merger therefore cannot eliminate the vulnerability while membership
remains unbounded.

\subsection{Exact and state-free attack costs}\label{sec:paddingcost}


For the arithmetic mean, let $S_t=\sum_{i\in G_t}e_i$ and $m_t=\lvert G_t\rvert$ for the group tested
at step $t$. After $r$ zero-evidence additions, its evidence is $S_t/(m_t+r)$. If the original group
is rejected at level $\alphat$, the minimum number of additions needed to suppress that rejection is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rstar}
r^{\star}_t=\lfloor S_t\alphat\rfloor-m_t+1 ,
\end{equation}
where we write $r^{\star}$ when the step is clear. The traffic construction is black-box---ordinary
connections on a common service to the host already under attack---but exact sizing requires $S_t$,
the live level $\alphat$, and the unperturbed controller trajectory. Thus $r^{\star}_t$ is the oracle
per-alert cost defined in \cref{sec:threat}.

We report three complementary cost summaries. For the baseline true detections $\mathcal D$,
$r^{\star}_t$ prices one alert on the unperturbed trajectory and
$\sum_{t\in\mathcal D}r^{\star}_t$ adds those per-alert costs. The campaign-level quantity
$J_{\mathrm{seq}}$ is the total paid by a greedy sequential oracle that re-evaluates each episode on
the attacked trajectory. A \statefree\ attacker instead uses a fixed multiplier $c$ of its own episode
size. \Cref{tab:costcurve} reports all three summaries.

A \statefree\ attacker can over-provision from its own episode size alone. Multiplying an episode by
$c$, through $(c-1)m_t$ additions, suppresses its alert whenever
$c\ge1+r^{\star}_t/m_t$. No live access to $S_t$, $\alphat$, or $\nCal$ is required; on LSPR23, $c=10$
is the smallest tested multiplier in $\{2,3,5,10,100\}$ that suppresses every canonical,
horizon-free alert at seed 0 in the primary and stress windows, the two arms with stored per-episode
arities. The measured cost is higher than the exact minimum. At the secondary window, one canonical
alert with $m_t=22$ and $r^{\star}_t=859$ needs $c=41$. For horizon-aware spending, the joint rerun
in \cref{sec:costcurve} yields an exact, sharper result.

\begin{corollary}[Horizon-uniform feasibility ratio as a dilution threshold]\label{cor:dilution}
Run e-LOND under the horizon-uniform allocation, so that $\alphat=(\alpha/T)(R_{t-1}+1)$ with
$R_0=0$, on arithmetic-mean group evidence whose flow e-values are bounded by $\ceil$, and write
$\rho=\rho_{\text{e-LOND}}=\ceil\alpha/T$, the horizon-uniform ratio \eqref{eq:rho} for e-LOND. Suppose no hypothesis other than the attacker's own
episodes carries evidence at or above the cold-start threshold $T/\alpha$, and the attacker
multiplies every one of its own episodes by an integer $c\ge1$, appending $(c-1)m_t$ zero-evidence
flows to an episode of arity $m_t$. If $c>\rho$, the attacked trajectory is rejection-free. If
$c\le\rho$ and some attacker episode attains $\ceil$, it is not rejection-free. Hence the smallest integer
multiplier that suffices for every stream satisfying the hypotheses is
$c_{\mathrm{int}}=\lfloor\rho\rfloor+1$.
\end{corollary}

For a particular stream, the smaller integer $\lfloor c_{\mathrm{crit}}\rfloor+1$ suffices, where
$c_{\mathrm{crit}}\le\rho$ is the largest attacker evidence multiplied by $\alpha/T$. Equality holds
when an attacker episode attains the ceiling, as occurs in every evaluated window; the proof is in
\cref{app:proofs}. Under the corollary's assumptions, the same ratio that governs cold-start
feasibility also gives the universal dilution threshold for attacker-owned cold-start alerts.
\Cref{app:ait} measures the cases in which the assumptions fail.

In the primary and secondary windows, $\rho$ is $2.13$ and $2.49$, so $c=3$ suffices and $c=2$ does
not: against the horizon-aware controller, multiplying \emph{every} own episode by $c=3$ silences it
at both windows. The one horizon-aware
false discovery in the secondary window occurs only after earlier
detections have raised its level, and disappears when those detections are suppressed, so the
corollary's condition on other hypotheses holds. The attacker needs only its own arity $m_t$. On
LSPR23, all 674 detected episodes lie on host pairs with no benign traffic, so that arity is simply the
number of flows the attacker generated. No estimate of $S_t$, $\alphat$, or $\nCal$ is needed.
\Cref{app:nonoracle} reports the over-provisioning factors, tested variants, and the one first-flow arm
for which no fixed multiplier suffices.

Because \eqref{eq:rstar} increases with $\alphat$, the offered level determines the cost of suppressing
an individual alert. Under horizon-free $\gamma\propto j^{-1.6}$, the primary window produces three
canonical alerts near the evidence ceiling, with a median suppression cost of 24 added flows. Under

alerts with no observed false discoveries; the median cost rises to $2{,}946$. The corresponding
feasibility ratios are $\rho=2.13$ and $2.49$ for the primary and secondary windows.

Summing the separately priced primary-window pads gives
$\sum_{t\in\mathcal D}r^{\star}_t=627{,}495$. This is an upper bound on the cost of jointly suppressing
that set, not a joint minimum: removing an early alert lowers e-LOND's rejection count, which lowers
the level and cost of later alerts. The secondary horizon-aware arm's one false discovery is not
included in its per-alert sum. Within the unbounded-membership model of \cref{thm:padding}, allocation
changes these finite costs but cannot remove padding vulnerability. A hard membership limit would
change that model; below we measure the resulting attack boundary, not a deployed admission policy.

We measure this feedback on the canonical, seed-0 trajectories for both windows by rerunning e-LOND
over the fully padded stream, using the zero-evidence model validated by the real-flow replay in
\cref{sec:replay}. Applying all per-alert pads and rerunning the controller under canonical order
leaves no true detection standing in either regime or window; the secondary window's horizon-aware
false discovery also disappears. The greedy sequential attacker is cheaper because it sizes each pad
against the level actually offered on the attacked run. With earlier alerts removed, later levels stay
at or near cold start.

Under canonical order at the primary window, this attacker pays $5{,}650$ flows in total rather than the per-alert sum of
$627{,}495$; at the secondary window, it pays $9{,}992$ against $977{,}567$. Across the alerts it
pads, the median pad is $48.5$ and $33$ flows rather than the per-alert horizon-aware medians of
$2{,}946$ and $3{,}908$, and $13$ and $4$ alerts need no pad at all because they existed only at levels
raised by earlier detections. Thus the high horizon-aware cost
is an individual-alert price created by the controller's own detection history. The greedy joint costs
remain one to two orders of magnitude above the horizon-free totals of $30$ and $859$, and they are
upper bounds on the minimum joint cost because they describe one oracle strategy.

The resulting group sizes locate the membership-cap boundary. Under horizon-free spending, the
median padded primary-window episode has arity 63, and 5\% of deployment episodes are at least that
large. Per-alert horizon-aware pads have median arity $2{,}989$, reached by only $0.22\%$ of
episodes. Once controller state is propagated, however, the attacker has median padded arity $91$ at
the primary window and $55$ at the secondary, sizes reached by $3.8\%$ and $4.3\%$ of episodes.

We therefore rerun the sequential attacker under a capacity constraint: it pads an episode only when
$m_t+r_t\le n$ and otherwise leaves the original alert in place. This experiment does not truncate or
rescore the complete clean and attacked streams, so it is not an end-to-end evaluation of an
admission-control policy. Under canonical order and $n=100$, $96$ of
the $105$ horizon-aware primary-window alerts still fire: $44$ because their pad would not fit even at
the cold-start level, and $52$ because those first alerts raise $R$ enough to price later pads above
the cap; at the secondary window $79$ of $107$ fire ($33$ and $46$) by the same mechanisms. At a cap
of 300, we observe $19$ and $60$ alerts firing, whereas the per-alert reading where
$m_t+r^{\star}_t>n$ predicts $91$ and $95$, and at $1{,}000$ the sequential attacker silences both
windows. A cap of 100 would alter
$3.4\%$ and $2.9\%$ of benign episodes as currently formed. This is the share an implemented cap
would alter, not a measurement of its effect on evidence, clean detection, or benign service.

The same constraint does not stop the horizon-free attacker: all three primary-window alerts remain
suppressible at $n\ge100$. Reaching them requires $n=30$, which would alter $7.4\%$ of benign
episodes. Thus the experiment identifies a mitigation boundary under an enforced capacity model; it
does not establish the performance of a working defence.


\subsection{Real-flow replay validates the cost model}\label{sec:replay}

At the running levels received by each episode, the three canonical primary-window alerts require 23,
24, and 33 zero-evidence additions; the eleven canonical secondary-window alerts have a median cost of six
(\cref{fig:attacks,tab:main}). Those costs are arithmetic on stored evidence. To test them against the implemented detector, we build
a weak-attacker pool using only network-observable service frequency. Specifically, we select the most
common protocol--port pair in the training prefix, before either calibration or deployment. The
selection uses no attack labels, detector outputs, calibration scores, or deployment traffic.

None of 20,000 sampled pool flows reaches the primary window's conformal tail, giving a one-sided 95\%
upper bound of $1.5\times10^{-4}$ on the firing probability. Injecting these real flows suppresses all
$3/3$ canonical primary-window detections at exactly the predicted $23$--$33$-flow thresholds. It also suppresses
all $11/11$ secondary-window detections, with measured cost equal to the closed form for every episode
and median cost six.

The horizon-aware regime replays the same way: per-alert replay suppresses $105/105$ and $107/107$
true detections in the primary and secondary windows at their original levels, on every draw. The baseline runs have
no false discovery in the primary window and one in the secondary (realised FDP $0.009$). A firing pad
flow would contribute the full evidence ceiling, but none of the $845{,}691$ available primary-window
pool flows reaches the tail. This full-pool zero count tightens the one-sided 95\% upper bound to
$3.5\times10^{-6}$ and makes the replay cost equal, rather than approximate, to the zero-evidence
closed form for this finite pool. The interval treats future pads as draws from a common firing rate;
it is not a distribution-free guarantee for future operational traffic. \Cref{app:pools} quantifies
the remaining risk. Across both windows, replay covers fourteen
horizon-free alerts at recalls $0.01$ and $0.03$, and 212 horizon-aware true detections at recalls
$0.38$ and $0.27$.

Replay evaluates each alert separately at its level on the unperturbed trajectory. It measures
e-values with real traffic and the implemented detector, then applies the aggregation and controller
arithmetic at that fixed level. Its purpose is to establish the zero-evidence pad model; the joint rerun in
\cref{sec:costcurve} propagates those pads through the altered e-LOND state.

LSPR23 provides no ordinary traffic on attacked host pairs, which are 100\% malicious. Its pool is
therefore service-level: flows matching the modal training-prefix protocol and port across the window,
rather than flows sent to a particular victim. The AIT experiment below closes this gap using actual
benign-to-victim traffic. Results are largely insensitive to the eligible pool
(\cref{app:pools}), but they do depend on when the pool is selected: choosing the modal service from the
deployment window can select the attacker's own high-scoring flood, which does not dilute evidence.

Ordering changes which alerts are issued, not the conditional result. Every alert produced under any
of the fifty audited pre-committed orders has a finite exact cost under
\eqref{eq:rstar} (\cref{app:padwindows}).

\section{Design Guidance and Evaluated Boundaries}\label{sec:fixes}

\subsection{Where a mitigation must act}

\Cref{thm:padding} identifies the design boundary. Under attacker-controlled, unbounded membership,
replacing one alert-capable symmetric merger with another cannot provide padding robustness. A mitigation must instead control membership or admission,
change the calibrated unit, or introduce asymmetry. \Cref{tab:defenses} evaluates one representative
from each route; each exchanges one property for another. The membership-cap experiment makes this
boundary especially clear: under horizon-aware spending, preventing a required pad from fitting can
leave an early alert in place, and that rejection raises the cost of later pads. Under horizon-free
spending, the tested caps do not stop the attack. Because we constrain only the attacker's additions
and do not apply a truncation policy to the complete stream, these results locate a capacity boundary;
they do not demonstrate the clean or attacked performance of an operational admission-control defence.


\subsection{A deployment checklist}

The evaluated boundaries reduce to five checks a deployment can make before and during operation.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Compute $\rho$ before deployment.} Given $\nCal$, $k$, and $T$, \cref{cor:budget} decides
whether a covered controller can report an alert at all across the intended horizon. If calibration
cannot support the horizon, a better-ranking detector cannot repair the shortfall, and switching among
the procedure--sequence pairs of \cref{thm:family1,thm:family2} leaves the boundary where it is.
\item \textbf{Do not group on a key the attacker can cheaply co-occupy.} The grouping key sets the
horizon, the meaning of an alert, and the attack cost together (\cref{sec:granularity}); report the
calibration-to-hypothesis ratio and a fixed-denominator resolution measure such as alert blur alongside
recall.
\item \textbf{Treat membership limits and admission as part of the threat model.} Any field that
determines group membership is attacker-facing, as is state used to set future testing levels
(\cref{app:state}). A per-host-pair cap is a capacity boundary, not a demonstrated defence, until the
clean and attacked streams have been evaluated end to end under it.
\item \textbf{If restarting, say which guarantee holds.} A pre-committed split $\sum_iq_i\le q$ keeps the
deployment-wide budget and recovers less power; a fresh $q$ per epoch recovers more but promises only a
per-epoch $\FDR$ (\cref{app:feasibility}).
\item \textbf{Do not expect a different symmetric merger to fix padding.} Under attacker-controlled,
unbounded membership, \cref{thm:padding} rules that out; a mitigation has to act on membership, on the
calibrated unit, or through asymmetry, and each route trades something away (\cref{tab:defenses}). A
metadata-keyed order stops one padded group from shifting unrelated hypotheses but does not stop
padding, and a keyed hash only changes the cost of insertion (\cref{sec:transfer}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Empirical breadth.} LSPR23 supplies the main chronology, with correlated windows from one
live-fire exercise (\cref{sec:data}). Position 0.85 is the known-invalid stress window and supports only
mechanism and cost measurements. AIT-LDSv2.0 adds a benign-inclusive transfer setting, but not broad
cross-domain validation. Because LSPR23 has no reliable flow-to-campaign identifier, our operational
units are episodes; the five-minute atom is a stable evaluation denominator, not externally validated
incident ground truth.

\emph{Attack cost.} Per-alert cost depends on the configuration that produced the alert because
\eqref{eq:rstar} scales with the offered level. The joint rerun covers two windows and one seed, and
assumes that the attacker knows which episodes it owns and that its pads carry zero evidence. On
LSPR23, labels provide ownership and attacked host pairs contain no benign traffic. A positive pad
firing rate would increase $J_{\mathrm{seq}}$; the complete primary-window pool contains no firing flow, giving a
one-sided 95\% upper bound of $3.5\times10^{-6}$, but the joint rerun does not sample that residual
risk. The class-wide result is finite cost, not equal cost or available cover in every deployment.
On AIT, a \statefree\ attacker sees only a lower bound on $m_t$; the deployment pool is retrospective,
and the temporally restricted prior pool can still contain traffic invisible to a compromised source.
Joint totals sum per-organisation medians, not one campaign trajectory; \cref{apptab:joint76rel}
gives the per-trajectory reliability behind them.

\emph{Detector and grouping scope.} The host-conditioned experiment covers six causal features and our
specific accumulation model for an already-active attacker--victim pair with fixed distinct-peer
counts. It does not characterise every host-aware detector, padding from many new peers, or a
high-volume flood. The AIT flow-only result uses canonical order with first-flow sensitivity; the
host-conditioned arm covers all eight organisations under first-flow order only
(\cref{tab:main}). LSPR23 joint reruns use zero-evidence pads, whereas AIT joint runs sample real pads;
the \texttt{wilson} host costs are upper bounds.

\emph{Capacity model.} The cap constrains attacker additions; it is not applied to the full stream.
The benign shares count episodes an implemented policy would alter, but we do not recompute evidence,
clean detection, false discoveries, or disruption. This is a capacity boundary, not a demonstrated defence.

\emph{Theorem scope.} The finite horizon covers the procedure--sequence pairs in
\cref{sec:horizon} under uninterrupted operation, together with the sparse-evidence closure variants.
Smoothing, restart, deferred decisions, different evidence constructions, and faster-growing history
terms change either the mechanism or its guarantee. The result does not imply that online FDR control
is unusable in general. \Cref{app:state}\label{sec:stateptr} separately extends the analysis to ADDIS's
evidence-conditioned spending index.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Online FDR and finite conformal resolution.} LOND, LORD, SAFFRON, ADDIS, e-LOND, online e-BH,
and e-GAI provide online error guarantees under different evidence and dependence
assumptions~\cite{javanmard2015lond,javanmard2018lord,ramdas2017lordpp,ramdas2018saffron,tian2019addis,xu2024elond,wang2022evaluebh,fischer2025onlineebh,egai2025}.
They build on $\alpha$-investing~\cite{foster2008alpha}, safe testing~\cite{grunwald2024safe}, and
offline FDR control~\cite{benjamini1995controlling}. Alpha-death and power loss at small levels are
well known~\cite{ramdas2017lordpp,foster2008alpha}; mem-e-LORD~\cite{egai2025}, for example, matches
e-LORD before the first rejection.

Huo et al.~\cite{huo2024realtime} observe how the conformal floor affects LOND, SAFFRON, and ADDIS,
while Hennhofer and Preisach~\cite{hennhofer2026resolution} study resolution collapse and smoothing in
low-data batch tests. Our theorems turn this interaction into a deployment boundary: given $\nCal$,
$k$, and $T$, they determine whether any admissible observation can be rejected at the intended
horizon for two controller families. \Cref{cor:budget} shows that the horizon-uniform boundary is
attained, and the resulting criterion also classifies recent closure and donation
constructions~\cite{xu2026eclosure,xu2025closure}. Kr\"onert et al.~\cite{kronert2024fdr} derive the related equality
$n=\nu m/\alpha-1$ to tune calibration for an exact realised level in a windowed procedure. Our result
instead marks the infeasibility boundary of one uninterrupted controller; their windowing corresponds
to the restart alternative in \cref{app:feasibility}.

\textbf{Trustworthy intrusion detection and temporal evaluation.} TESSERACT's temporal evaluation
principles~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract} and conformal drift systems such as
Transcend(ent)~\cite{jordaney2017transcend,barbero2022transcendent} motivate our chronological design.
The closest systems implement subsets of our pipeline. Rebjock et al.~\cite{rebjock2021online} apply
online FDR to time-series anomaly statistics; C-PP-COAD~\cite{farzaneh2025coad} adds conformal p-values
and active statistics, but not attacker-influenceable metadata groups. Online multi-layer
FDR~\cite{wang2025multilayer} partitions hypotheses rather than merging observations. CALIBURN's
streaming intrusion pipeline uses empirical calibration, cost-sensitive thresholds, within-window
conformal risk control, and burn-rate alerts~\cite{caliburn2026}; Gao et al.~\cite{gao2026boundary}
combine conformal novelty detection with batch BH and boundary-FDR procedures. Neither is an
arrival-time FDR controller over merged e-values. We therefore treat the full stack as an evaluated
design assembled from established components, not as an existing deployment. Because label quality is a persistent
concern~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting,liu2022error}, we also audit against an external record
(\cref{app:validity}).

\textbf{E-merging and adversarial testing.} E-merging theory characterises admissible
symmetric~\cite{vovk2021evalues} and asymmetric~\cite{wang2025admissible} rules at fixed arity. Our padding
adversary acts across arities. It neither changes detector scores nor corrupts reports, unlike attacks
based on manipulated scores, perturbed p-values, or Byzantine
inputs~\cite{yasodharan2019nonzero,chen2024adversarialbh,zhang2025conformalevasion,zhang2025byzantine}.
Instead, it adds legitimate observations to its own tested hypothesis. \Cref{thm:padding} lifts this
mechanism from one implementation to the entire alert-capable symmetric class.

Batching can improve online-testing power~\cite{zrnic2020batching}, but standard results require
within-batch independence or positive dependence that co-located flows sharing a calibration tail do
not provide. We therefore use decision deadlines~\cite{fisher2022deadlines,xu2026eclosure}, which are
compatible with arbitrary dependence; batching under one deployment-wide budget still obeys
\cref{cor:budget}. Feedback-aware procedures incorporate revealed labels with finite-sample
control~\cite{lu2026gaif,liu2025ocsarc}; \cref{app:baselines} evaluates simpler threshold controllers under
analyst delay.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A statistically valid trust layer can still be operationally silent or adversarially suppressible.
For the controller families studied here, finite evidence and arrival-time spending create a
calculable, absorbing discovery horizon; calibration must grow at least linearly with the number of
tested hypotheses. Grouping restores feasibility at that best-case rate,
but an attacker-co-occupiable key turns group membership into a control surface. Under unbounded,
attacker-controlled membership, every alert-capable symmetric e-merging family yields to sufficient
zero-evidence padding.

Controller allocation sets the price of an individual alert, not the existence of the vulnerability.
In our joint reruns, suppressing early alerts keeps later levels near cold start and makes campaign cost
far smaller than the per-alert sum. The AIT capability arms also distinguish finite algebraic cost from
a temporally restricted, low-information attack: template availability and observability determine the
realised suppression. Evidence scale, controller spending, alert granularity, and membership control therefore
require joint design; a membership cap requires end-to-end evaluation before it can be called an
effective defence.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

This paper describes working attacks against a statistical trust layer for intrusion detection, so we
state our reasoning against the Menlo Report framing~\cite{menlo2012}.

\emph{Respect for persons and justice.} All measurements are made on public research datasets released
for this purpose, captured during authorised cyber-defence exercises on purpose-built ranges rather than
on production networks carrying third-party traffic. We rely on the publishers' own ethical review and
release terms and did not conduct a separate consent audit, so we make no claim about individual consent
beyond what those releases state. No attack was executed against any live network, and no personal data
is used, disclosed or re-identified.

\emph{Beneficence and responsible disclosure.} This work does not target a deployed product, and the
class of system it studies is, to our knowledge, not yet widely deployed, which is the point at which
disclosure is cheapest and most useful. It introduces no new attack on the underlying detector: it
identifies a security consequence that emerges when public statistical components are composed ---
bounded evidence, attacker-influenceable grouping and online error spending together produce an alert
layer that can become infeasible or suppressible. We report both the inexpensive and the expensive
attack regimes and evaluate representative mitigations, so that designers can assess the exposure
before deploying such a composition.

\emph{Law and public interest.} We identify a small set of host pairs whose labels we believe to be wrong
and deliberately do not publish corrected labels: rewriting a public dataset's labels from our own
detector's tail would be circular, and the pairs were identified using test-split information. We report
the direction of the error instead.

\subsection{The group-validity premise}\label{app:groupval}

Using the mean at variable, metadata-selected arity needs a group-level premise, and different
controllers need different things of it: e-LOND and online e-BH control $\FDR$ under arbitrary
dependence given only \emph{marginally} valid group e-values, whereas the p-value procedures need
conditional super-uniformity, independence or monotonicity. The guarantee-bearing claims therefore use
e-LOND.

The conditioning is stated \emph{per hypothesis}. For group $j$ let $\mathcal M_j$ be the
$\sigma$-field generated by the pre-committed metadata that determines that hypothesis and nothing else:
the grouping key, the membership indicators and arity $m_j$, the group's position in the pre-committed
within-bucket order (hence the spending weight $\gamma_j$ it is offered), and the horizon $T$. It
carries no information about any other group's composition. Pre-committed means chosen before the scores
and not score-adaptive.

\begin{assumption}[Group-local metadata-conditional validity]\label{assump:groupval}
For every benign flow $i$ of a true-null group $G_j$, $\mathbb E[e_i\mid\mathcal M_j]\le1$; for the
two-point e-value, equivalently $\Pr(K_i\le k\mid\mathcal M_j)\le k/(\nCal+1)$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{lemma}[Group-level marginal e-validity]\label{lem:groupval}
Under \cref{assump:groupval}, every true-null group with $m_j>0$ has a marginally valid group e-value,
$\mathbb E[\Ev(G_j)]\le1$, for random membership and arity and under arbitrary within- and between-group
dependence.
\end{lemma}

Conditioning on $\mathcal M_j$ fixes membership and $m_j$, so linearity gives
$\mathbb E[\Ev(G_j)\mid\mathcal M_j]\le1$ and the tower property removes the conditioning (proof
below). The premise is stronger than the unconditional validity split conformal supplies, it is a
genuine modelling assumption (endpoint and service metadata can correlate with the timing and volume
features that drive the score), and it is \emph{not} established by the dataset. \Cref{tab:scope}
separates every claim by what it needs.

\emph{A construction in which the premise holds and the attack still succeeds.} The coexistence the
paper claims---valid true-null evidence and a suppressible alert in the same run---can be exhibited
without data. Draw $\nCal=99$ calibration scores and the benign test scores independently from
$\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$, take $k=1$, so $\ceil=100$, and fix the groups and the within-bucket order in
advance, independently of every score. Run e-LOND under the horizon-uniform allocation with $T=4$
hypotheses and $\alpha=0.05$: the cold-start level is $\alpha/T=0.0125$ and the threshold is $80$.
Place a singleton attack flow with score $2$ first. It exceeds every calibration score, so $K=1$,
$e=100\ge80$, and it is rejected. Now let the attacker append one flow of its own with score $-1$ to
that episode: the flow ranks below every calibration score, $K=100>k$, its e-value is $0$, and the
group mean becomes $50<80$, so the alert is suppressed; nothing else in the stream has changed.
\Cref{assump:groupval} holds by construction before and after the pad. Every true-null group is
untouched by the attack, its membership, arity and position were fixed before the scores were drawn, so
conditioning on $\mathcal M_j$ conditions on nothing that depends on the scores, and each benign score
is exchangeable with the calibration scores, giving
$\Pr(K_i\le1\mid\mathcal M_j)=1/(\nCal+1)$ and $\mathbb E[e_i\mid\mathcal M_j]=1$ exactly. By
\cref{lem:groupval} every true-null group e-value is marginally valid and the nominal $\FDR$ guarantee
is intact, while the one alert the guarantee was meant to deliver has been removed by a single flow.
The validity of the nulls and the suppressibility of the alert are properties of different objects,
and neither constrains the other; \cref{sec:attack} measures the same separation on real streams.

\subsection{Exact scope of the finite-horizon theorems}\label{app:horizonscope}

\Cref{thm:family1} covers LOND, e-LOND and e-LORD, the last through the equivalence e-LORD $=$ e-LOND
under $\gamma_t=\omega_t\prod_{j<t}(1-\omega_j)$~\cite{egai2025}. That equivalence is a hypothesis and
not a remark, and it needs two things of the weights, not one. They must be \emph{deterministic
functions of the index}, fixed before deployment --- predictability in the filtration, which is all
online FDR normally asks, would make $\gamma$ a history-dependent random object, and
\cref{thm:family1}'s condition is a statement about a sequence. And they must satisfy
$\omega_j\in[0,1]$: pre-commitment alone gives fixedness only, and a pre-committed $\omega_1=2$ yields
$\gamma_1=2$ and $\gamma_2<0$, breaking both $\gamma\ge0$ and the budget. Under
$\omega_j\in[0,1]$ the partial sums telescope, $\sum_{t\le n}\gamma_t=1-\prod_{j\le n}(1-\omega_j)\le1$,
which gives \cref{thm:family1} the non-negativity and the budget. It does not give the decay
condition, and the sparse sequence of the next paragraph is itself induced by a deterministic
$\omega\in[0,1]$, so $\gamma_t t^d\to0$ has to be checked for the induced $\gamma$, as it is for the
$\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$ pair we run.

\Cref{thm:family2} covers LORD++ ($a_1=\alpha-w_0$, $a_j=\alpha$ for $j\ge2$). What is covered is a
procedure \emph{together with} its spending sequence, not a named procedure in the abstract:
summability alone is not enough for \cref{thm:family1} at $d=1$, since
$\gamma_{2^n}=n^{-2}/\zeta(2)$ for $n\ge1$, with $\gamma_t=0$ elsewhere, sums to exactly one yet has
$2^n\gamma_{2^n}\to\infty$. That example refutes the \emph{hypothesis}, not the conclusion: the same
sequence has $\gamma_t\to0$ and so is still absorbed at $d=0$. The decay condition therefore has to be
checked per pair, and it is asymptotic: for $\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$, $t\gamma_t\to0$ so $d=1$ holds,
whereas a level growing quadratically in the rejection count would escape the argument. A
horizon-uniform $\gamma$ is supported on $t\le T$, so an asymptotic decay condition is met trivially
and decides nothing; it needs none, because before any rejection the level is the constant $c_0/T$
across the horizon, feasibility there is all-or-nothing, and \cref{cor:budget} decides it exactly.

The two families are not equally strong, and the difference is worth stating. \Cref{thm:family1}'s
bound $\alphat\le\alpha c_g\gamma_t t^d$ uses only $R_{t-1}\le t-1$, so it holds whatever the history:
the feasible set is a fixed set of indices and absorption does not depend on the run being
rejection-free. \Cref{thm:family2}'s bound is driven by the length $\Delta$ of the \emph{current}
rejection-free gap, so its absorption is conditional on such a gap occurring. A lag-sum procedure that
keeps rejecting keeps its level up --- for LORD++ under $\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$ with a rejection at
every step, $\alphat$ climbs from its cold-start value towards $0.039$, and every step of that run is
feasible once $\nCal\ge91$, the binding step being the cold start itself. This is why the
paper's claims are cold-start claims: the horizon is the horizon of a rejection-free prefix, which is
the state a deployment starts in and, on these streams, mostly stays in. The theorems are scoped
deliberately: they do not say every online FDR procedure dies, and \cref{app:feasibility} locates the
procedures that escape.

\subsection{Attainment and the symmetric class}\label{app:attain}

Every rejection threshold in our setting is $\theta=1/\alphat>1$, and attainment is the hypothesis the
deployment supplies whenever an alert is possible at all: if a symmetric family ever issues an alert then
some input it receives has $F\ge\theta$, so it attains $\theta$ and \cref{thm:padding} applies to it.
\emph{Not} being $\theta$-padding-robust and attaining $\theta$ are therefore the same condition, since a
family reaching $\theta$ nowhere --- $F\equiv1$, which is padding-invariant but can never fire, because
rejection needs evidence $\ge1/\alphat>1$ --- satisfies \cref{def:padding} vacuously. For the mean rule
the body evaluates the witness is explicit: the all-firing group $x=(\ceil,\ldots,\ceil)$ attains
$\theta$ at every evidence-feasible step \eqref{eq:feas}. The implication runs only that way. Attainment is strictly the weaker condition, since it quantifies over
all of $[0,\infty)^m$ --- a scaled rule such as $\tfrac12\mathrm{mean}$ is a valid symmetric e-merging
family that attains $\theta$ somewhere yet, on two-point evidence with $\theta>\ceil/2$, can never fire
--- so what attainment excludes is only the rules that reach $\theta$ nowhere, which is all the theorem
needs. Ordinary domination by the mean is false ($\kappa+(1-\kappa)\,\mathrm{mean}$, for
$\kappa\in(0,1)$, is a counterexample), which is why the proof splits cases.

\subsection{Further results used in the body}\label{app:further}

\begin{proposition}[Donation is bounded before the first rejection]\label{prop:donation}
For donation e-LOND with parameter $\delta\in(0,1)$ and a spending sequence with $\gamma\ge0$ and
$\sum_t\gamma_t\le1$ --- the normalisation the bound relies on --- on a rejection-free prefix
$\alphat\le\delta\gamma_t/(1-\delta)$. The restriction $\delta<1$ is necessary, not cosmetic: at
$\delta=1$ the donated wealth can drive the denominator to zero and hold $\alphat$ away from zero
along a rejection-free run, so neither the bound nor absorption survives (\cref{app:proofs}). Hence donation e-LOND satisfies \cref{thm:family1}'s hypotheses \emph{along a rejection-free prefix},
with $c_g=\delta/[\alpha(1-\delta)]$ --- which is $1/(1-\delta)$ exactly when $\delta=\alpha$, the setting
we run, since \cref{thm:family1} writes the level as $\alpha\gamma_t g$ --- and $d=0$, because
that bound holds uniformly in $t$ --- $g$ itself is not constant along the prefix, since the donated
wealth grows, but $d=0$ asks for a constant bound, not a constant $g$. The instantiation matters: $d=0$ asks only for
$\gamma_t\to0$, which summability supplies, whereas $d=1$ would ask for $t\gamma_t\to0$, which it does
not (take $\gamma_{2^j}=2^{-j}$ and $\gamma_t=0$ elsewhere). That is all \cref{thm:family1} needs, since its conclusion is
itself a statement about rejection-free runs --- so the horizon stays finite and the run is absorbed. It is
\emph{not} a global member of the family: past $1/\delta$ rejections no finite $c_g(R+1)^d$ bounds the
level. The bound is a
cold-start statement only --- past $1/\delta$ rejections the denominator can vanish --- and it is sharp
where it applies. On our streams the realised donation wealth stays at most $0.12$ of a budget of $1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Closure is bounded by its zero-evidence subset]\label{prop:closure}
For closed e-LOND whose subset aggregator is zero on all-zero input --- $E_S=0$ whenever $E_i=0$ for
every $i\in S$, including $S=\emptyset$ --- let $Z_t=\#\{i<t:E_i=0\}$. Then
$\alphat\le\delta\gamma_{Z_t+1}(R_{t-1}+1)$. The hypothesis is not implied by e-merging validity:
$\kappa+(1-\kappa)\,\mathrm{mean}$ is symmetric and valid yet returns $\kappa>0$ on all-zero input,
and the zero-evidence subset then certifies nothing.
\end{proposition}

\begin{corollary}[When the closure is absorbing]\label{cor:closureabsorb}
Let $H=\min\{j:\gamma_j<1/(\delta\ceil)\}$ be the first index at which the spending weight dips below
the floor --- \cref{thm:family1}'s index horizon when $\gamma$ is decreasing, as ours is --- and
$P_T=\#\{i\le T:E_i>0\}$ the number of hypotheses carrying any evidence. \emph{(i)} Along a
rejection-free run, closed e-LOND is absorbed by step $H+P_T$ whenever $H+P_T\le T$: an additive offset,
holding pathwise with no assumption beyond counting. \emph{(ii)} Asymptotically, if
$\liminf_t Z_t/t>0$ then for every $\rho'<\liminf_t Z_t/t$ there is an onset $t_1$ beyond which the
closure's effective spending index is at least $\rho' t$, so absorption occurs by
$\max\{t_1,\lceil(H-1)/\rho'\rceil\}$ --- the index is rescaled by $\rho'$ and the absorption
\emph{time} therefore stretched by $1/\rho'$, not shortened. A positive $\liminf$ permits an
arbitrarily long initial transient, which is why $t_1$ cannot be dropped. On our streams $P_T\in\{0,\ldots,152\}$, at most $0.48\%$ of
$T$, so form (i) applies and the offset is at most 152 hypotheses.
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[Reach]\label{thm:reach}
Fix one hypothesis, offered level $\alphat$, whose episode has flow positions $i$. For a pre-committed
weight sequence with $\sum_iw_i\le1$, the number $P$ of positions at which a lone attack flow can fire
the rule $F(e)=\sum_iw_ie_i$ is at most $\alphat\ceil$, the quantity that governs \cref{cor:budget}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Front-load cost]\label{thm:frontload}
At that same hypothesis, detection is impossible once $L\ge L^{\star}(w):=\min\{L:\sum_{i>L}w_i<\beta\}$,
with $\beta=1/(\alphat\ceil)$; $L^{\star}$ is finite for every summable $w$. The threshold is \emph{sufficient}
for impossibility, and exact only when every non-prepended position carries firing evidence: with fewer
firing flows detection can already be impossible at smaller $L$, so $L^{\star}$ is an upper bound on what
the attacker must prepend.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Procedure definitions}

Each procedure offers a level $\alphat$ and rejects $H_t$ when the evidence exceeds $1/\alphat$
(p-value procedures reject when $p_t\le\alphat$). We write $R_{t-1}$ for the rejection count, $\tau_j$
for the $j$-th rejection time, and, for the candidate-based procedures, $C_{j+}(t)$ for the number of
candidates after $\tau_j$. Transcriptions were unit-tested against literal $O(T^2)$ implementations of
the published formulas and against four identities the sources state (offline e-BH as the $t=K$ case of
online e-BH; $R(\text{e-LOND})\subseteq R(\text{online e-BH})$; e-LORD $=$ e-LOND under
$\gamma_t=\omega_t\prod_{j<t}(1-\omega_j)$; e-SAFFRON$(\lambda{=}0)=$e-LORD).
{\small\begin{align*}
\text{LOND, e-LOND:}\ &\alphat=\alpha\,\gamma_t\,(R_{t-1}+1),\\
\text{LORD++:}\ &\alphat=\gamma_t w_0+(\alpha-w_0)\gamma_{t-\tau_1}+\alpha\!\sum_{j\ge2}\gamma_{t-\tau_j},\\
\text{SAFFRON:}\ &\alphat=\min\big\{\lambda,\,(1-\lambda)[w_0\gamma_{t-C_{0+}}+\cdots]\big\},\\
\text{ADDIS:}\ &\alphat=\min\big\{\lambda,\,(\tau-\lambda)[w_0\gamma_{S^t-C_{0+}}+\cdots]\big\},\\
\text{online e-BH:}\ &\text{reject } H_i \text{ iff } E_i\ge 1/(k^\star_t\alpha\gamma_i),\\
\text{e-LORD (e-GAI):}\ &\alphat=\omega_t\big(\alpha-\textstyle\sum_{j<t}\tfrac{a_j}{R_{j-1}+1}\big)(R_{t-1}+1).
\end{align*}}%
SAFFRON (serial estimate of the alpha fraction that is futilely rationed on true null hypotheses) and
ADDIS (adaptive discarding) are the adaptive descendants of LORD, and online e-BH is the e-value
Benjamini--Hochberg procedure run at each step. SAFFRON's candidates are the $p\le\lambda$; ADDIS additionally \emph{selects} the $p\le\tau$ and counts
selected non-candidates. Here $k^\star_t=\max\{k\le t:\sum_{j\le t}\mathbf{1}\{E_j\ge
1/(k\alpha\gamma_j)\}\ge k\}$ is the online-e-BH step-up fixed point, and $\omega_t$ is the e-GAI weight
update~\cite{egai2025}, which we run \textbf{pre-committed} --- the specialisation under which the
e-LORD reparametrisation of \cref{sec:feasibility} yields a fixed summable $\gamma$, and the only one
our feasibility coverage claims. Throughout $\alpha=q=0.05$, $w_0=0.025$, $k=1$; ADDIS uses
$\lambda=0.25,\tau=0.5$, SAFFRON $\lambda=0.5$, e-SAFFRON $\lambda=0.1$, and e-GAI $\phi=\psi=0.5$,
$\omega_1=1/T$. The two spending sequences are $\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$ (normalised by
$\zeta(1.6)=2.2858$, no horizon needed) and horizon-uniform $\gamma_j=1/T$ (max-min optimal, \oracle).
We do not extend the feasibility bound to e-SAFFRON at $\lambda=0.1$, a testing level the equivalence
does not cover. mem-e-LORD is a different case and the tables mark it covered: its level has the family-I

rejections as $\FDR$-controlled. The r-LOND variants inherit the classification
verbatim, their rejection sets coinciding with e-LOND's on calibrated $p$-values. For e-TOAD we use the

\subsection{Reporting conventions}\label{app:conventions}

Unless a sentence, caption or table says otherwise, every measured number in this paper is detector
\textbf{seed 0} under the \textbf{canonical} within-bucket order at the stated window, in the
\textbf{horizon-free} regime $\gamma_j\propto j^{-1.6}$; horizon-aware results are named as such or
marked \oracle. Results that
necessarily run under first-flow arrival --- the alert-blur, smoothing, restart, asymmetric-weighting,
host-detector and ADDIS contrasts, and the AIT host-conditioned arm --- say so at the point of claim,
and their appendix tables carry both seeds where a seed applies. Every per-alert padding cost
$r^{\star}$ is an \emph{oracle lower bound}, because exact sizing reads the realised group evidence and
the live controller level; it is written $r^{\star}_t$ throughout, the subscript marking the step whose
running level prices it, and a row priced against the static cold-start threshold instead says so in
words and uses no such symbol (\cref{apptab:padpools,apptab:units}); whole-detected-set padding totals and the front-load cost $L^{\star}$ are
upper bounds, and the keyed-hash budgets $N_{50/90/99}$ are reliability figures over 400 draws
(\cref{apptab:blindkey}). Windows are named by chronological position: 0.55 primary, 0.62 secondary, 0.85 the
known-invalid stress window, used for mechanism and cost measurement only.

\subsection{Chronological split geometry}\label{app:splits}

The five deployment positions are overlapping chronological views of one exercise. Each deployment
block spans $[\mathrm{pos},\mathrm{pos}+0.15)N$ up to integer rounding, so adjacent
positions share $46.7$--$53.3\%$ of their deployment flows --- the primary/secondary pair shares
$53.3\%$, the largest in the set --- and the deployment block at one position coincides, to within one
flow, with the calibration block of the position $0.15$ later. Deployment blocks two or more positions
apart are disjoint, but no two positions are independent even then: the training pools $[0,i_1)$ are
nested, so every earlier position trains on a prefix of every later one's data and the fitted detectors
are correlated across all five. Each position is internally chronological --- training, then
calibration, then deployment --- so this couples the positions to one another without crossing any
single position's own split. This is why the paper reports the five windows separately and never pools
them, and why 0.62 is a correlated robustness check rather than confirmatory replication of 0.55.

\subsection{Datasets and preprocessing}

LSPR23~\cite{lspr23} (Zenodo 8042347, CC-BY-4.0) is a single CSV of $16{,}353{,}511$ flows; 89\% of them
fall in the final 25.6 hours, so window positions are chronological slices rather than random splits. The
detector reads \texttt{Protocol} plus 32 per-flow timing and volume statistics (columns $9$--$40$); the
grouping key reads \texttt{SrcIP}, \texttt{DstIP} (columns $2$--$3$) and, for the service and subnet
families, the ports (columns $4$--$5$); the alert audit of \cref{sec:tail} reads the annotation columns.
The dataset carries no flow-to-campaign labels: \texttt{Expoid\_dst} is empty for 1,630,732 of the
1,644,599 malicious flows, and \texttt{Category}, \texttt{Severity} and \texttt{SigID} are effectively
constant where present. Timestamps are in microseconds and $7{,}381{,}261$ rows arrive out of order, so
the pipeline sorts on load. With test-window start fraction $\mathrm{pos}$,
$i_2=\lfloor\mathrm{pos}\cdot N\rfloor$, $i_1=i_2-\lfloor0.15N\rfloor$ and
$i_3=\min(N,i_2+\lfloor0.15N\rfloor)$ give training $[0,i_1)$, calibration $[i_1,i_2)$ and deployment
$[i_2,i_3)$; benign subsampling is confined to $[0,i_1)$. A separate red-team task record (288
narratives, 83 machine-readable compromise reports, 295 timestamped step submissions) is used only as
external ground truth in \cref{app:granularity,app:validity}, never as a feature.

\emph{AIT-LDSv2.0.} The transfer tests use AIT-LDSv2.0~\cite{aitlds} (Zenodo 5789064, CC-BY-4.0), eight
organisation testbeds whose released packet captures we convert to per-organisation \texttt{tstat}
\texttt{tcp\_complete} netflows following the dataset's own recipe (45,001 attack flows). Each flow
carries the dataset's own attack-label string; we treat as malicious exactly the attack-step labels it
defines (\texttt{service\_scan}, \texttt{host\_discover\_dmz}/\texttt{\_local},
\texttt{online\_cracking}, \texttt{wpscan}, \texttt{dirb\_scan}, \texttt{upload\_rce\_shell}\,\ldots) by
exact string match, and as benign exactly the labels the dataset marks benign; flows whose label is
unknown or empty are dropped rather than assumed benign. Numeric columns are kept per fold only if they
are non-null on $>\!90\%$ of rows and non-constant; all identity fields and all absolute-time fields are
excluded so no fold can key on the capture epoch, and the six host-context features are the same strictly
causal ones as on LSPR23. Splits are chronological within each organisation: calibration uses benign
flows strictly preceding that organisation's first attack flow, deployment is everything from that
instant on.

\emph{Detectors and grouping.} The detector is HistGradientBoosting; an unsupervised Isolation Forest runs
alongside as a feasibility-versus-sufficiency probe, with the same margin yet tail reach $0.000$ at all
ten configurations. A group's evidence uses every flow in its time bucket, so its hypothesis is emitted
only when the bucket closes, using only observations available then. Groups closing in the same bucket
are sequenced by the canonical splitmix64 hash of the group's own $(\mathrm{SrcIP},\mathrm{DstIP},
\text{bucket})$ key, collisions broken on the key; the first-flow arrival order is retained as a labelled
upper bound. The headline grouping is not tuned on its reported window: under previous-window selection
and five leave-one-out folds it is near-worst of the 30 groupings feasible at every window, the cap
being the selection-stable choice while the grouping does not transfer (\cref{apptab:transfer}). Every
closed form is verified numerically against an independent implementation, and each measurement script
carries unit tests.

\subsection{Feasibility versus power, in full}\label{app:feasnotpower}

Under the canonical order the primary window yields three e-LOND detections out of 275 malicious
episodes and the secondary window eleven; at positions 0.70 and 0.77 no episode clears its own step
anywhere in the stream, so the canonical count there is zero (\cref{apptab:ordering} carries the full
five-window order matrix). The separation appears directly in paired contrasts. At position 0.70 both
detector seeds share $\nCal$, $T$ and the margin $+0.536$, yet under first-flow order one seed yields 30
detections and the other none. A host-conditioned detector raises AUROC from 0.916 to 0.954 at the
primary window but reduces e-LOND detections from 18 to two (first-flow, both legs). An Isolation Forest
has the same margin as the gradient-boosted detector and not one attack flow above the calibration
maximum at any of the ten window--seed cells. Ranking quality can move power; it cannot move the margin
in \eqref{eq:margin}.

Two configurations outside the headline pair corroborate that the grouped counts are a property of the
spending sequence rather than of the ceiling: e-LORD under the horizon-aware e-GAI weighting reaches 101
detections at the same window and seed with no false discoveries, and periodic restart at the same
grouping and the same deployment-wide budget reaches 55.

Low prevalence compounds the cold-start problem, and live-fire prevalence is the optimistic case.
Thinning malicious episodes to a target rate while replacing each with benign evidence from the same
window holds $T$, the margin and every survivor's index exactly fixed: at $\pi=10^{-4}$ the probability
that e-LOND \emph{ever} rejects falls to $0$--$40\%$ across the evaluated cells, mostly below $10\%$,
while the margin does not move (\cref{apptab:prevalence}). This is a sensitivity analysis, not a
counterfactual deployment, but it shows why a short discovery prefix is most damaging in exactly the
regime that motivates FDR control.

\subsection{What escapes the horizon, and what it costs}\label{app:taxonomy}

Among the procedures we study the boundary is sharp: the horizon binds a procedure whose spending index
advances on \emph{every} hypothesis. One that advances it only on tested hypotheses can escape that
bound; one that defers the decision falls outside the predicate altogether (\cref{tab:taxonomy}).
SAFFRON sits outside the letter of the theorems, since its index counts non-candidates, but does not
escape in practice (silent at 99.3\% of steps at the primary window, first-flow procedure matrix).
ADDIS advances its index only on hypotheses whose p-values are selected ($p\le\tau$) but not candidates
($p\le\lambda$); with two-point evidence at $k=1$ no observed episode lands in $(\lambda,\tau]$, so the
index stops advancing and the level stays high. That is a genuine escape from absorption, but on the
real stream it coincides with a violation of ADDIS's uniform-conservativeness premise, and
\cref{app:state} shows that conditioning the index on observed evidence also makes the state
attacker-controllable. Online e-BH's step-up threshold is a fixed point over the whole observed
history~\cite{xu2024elond,wang2022evaluebh,fischer2025onlineebh}, so it does not decide hypothesis $t$
against a level fixed at $t$: \eqref{eq:feas}, and with it the absorption predicate, is simply not
defined for it. What it escapes is the \emph{permanence} of an early infeasible prefix --- a later
observation raises the step-up count and can make a hypothesis already passed rejectable. It does not
escape bounded evidence: index $j$ is rejectable only if some attainable count $R$ gives
$R\alpha\gamma_j\ge1/\ceil$, so a horizon-$T$ run still leaves an index no step-up count can reach.
Decision deadlines interpolate between immediate e-LOND-style decisions and indefinite
reconsideration~\cite{fisher2022deadlines,xu2026eclosure}, and a bucket-close deadline is exactly the
batched architecture a SOC receives, valid under arbitrary dependence where a batched BH is not. What
deferral buys is latency, not feasibility: the fraction of hypotheses that cannot be rejected \emph{at
their own arrival} is $96.3\%$ at the primary window whether decisions are immediate, held to bucket
close, or never forced, because a step-up count of $R$ needs $R$ hypotheses to clear the bar at once and
at most $0.48\%$ of episodes carry any evidence. Under the canonical order it adds one detection in the
stress window and none elsewhere (\cref{apptab:uai26}).

Four constructions alter the boundary, but none removes the tradeoff for free, and \cref{tab:taxonomy}
prices each; the smoothing and restart contrasts are measured under first-flow order on both legs, so
the order shifts the level and not the sign. Two remove a premise. \emph{Randomised smoothing} dissolves
the absorbing state by replacing the discrete p-value with a $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$
draw~\cite{vovk2005alrw,hennhofer2026resolution}, but its surplus is a lottery: the episodes detected
with probability $\ge0.9$ are exactly the discrete ones, and the surplus does not survive
arbitrary-dependence-valid merging (\cref{apptab:smoothing}). \emph{Periodic restart} renews the
cold-start budget and recovers real power. Held at the headline grouping, the primary window, seed 0
and the \emph{same} deployment-wide budget $\sum_i q_i\le q=0.05$, restarting every two hours takes
e-LOND from 18 rejections to 55, recall $0.065$ to $0.200$, at a measured $\FDP$ of zero. Spending a
fresh $q$ per epoch instead buys more --- recall $0.360$ at a total budget of $0.25$ --- but that is a
per-epoch $\FDR$, a weaker promise than the deployment-wide one
(\cref{apptab:restart}, proof in \cref{app:proofs}). Restart forces that choice rather than removing
it, and the comparison has to hold grouping, seed and total budget fixed or it measures the wrong
thing. Two work within the premises: the recent closure and compound-e
donation constructions~\cite{xu2026eclosure,xu2025closure}, strict improvements over e-LOND under
arbitrary dependence, are bounded on a rejection-free prefix by a constant cold-start factor
($1/(1-\delta)$, a $5\%$ change against a $20\times$ shortfall) and by an additive horizon offset
respectively (\cref{prop:donation,prop:closure,cor:closureabsorb}), and buy $+0$ true detections at
every window and seed we run (\cref{apptab:uai26}, seed 0 shown). Among the elapsed-time procedures
bound by \cref{thm:family1,thm:family2}, the cold-start barrier is therefore a property of the evidence
rather than of the controller's sophistication.

\emph{The level-$w_0$ margin.} The appendix tables report feasibility on one cross-controller scale
rather than per procedure, so that a single column serves every row:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:margin}
\text{margin}=\ceil\,\frac{w_0}{T}-1=\frac{(\nCal+1)\,w_0}{kT}-1 .
\end{equation}
It is the body's $\rho$ of \eqref{eq:rho} rescaled, $\text{margin}=\rho_p c_{0,p}^{-1}w_0-1$, and for
e-LOND, whose $c_0=2w_0$, $\rho_{\text{e-LOND}}=2(\text{margin}+1)$. A negative margin therefore does
\emph{not} imply e-LOND infeasibility: e-LOND can still fire down to margin $-\tfrac12$, the rejection
rule \eqref{eq:reject} being inclusive. This is exactly why the body plots $\rho$ instead.

\emph{Feasibility inputs, window by window.} Across the five positions $\nCal$ is
$2{,}448{,}993$, $2{,}449{,}031$, $2{,}287{,}988$, $2{,}116{,}418$ and $1{,}813{,}113$ benign
calibration flows, against $T=57{,}368$, $49{,}267$, $37{,}231$, $31{,}672$ and $31{,}568$ two-hour
host-pair episodes, matching \cref{apptab:detection}. Within-bucket ordering changes the sequence,
not $T$. The two headline windows are the two largest calibration sets, and 0.62 exceeds
0.55 rather than repeating it.

\emph{Cold-start arithmetic.} The count ratio of \cref{cor:budget} is dataset-independent, but its
calendar form is not: for LORD++ the horizon-uniform requirement is $1.44\times10^{9}$ calibration flows
for one hour of a 10k-flows/s link and $3.46\times10^{10}$ for a day. Under $\gamma\propto j^{-1.6}$ the
feasible prefix before any rejection is $902/902/865/824/748$ steps at the five windows for e-LOND. At the $\pi=10^{-4}$ sensitivity point
that window almost always closes empty: the first true attack is not expected until index
$\approx10{,}000$, so a rejection arrives in time to bootstrap the controller with probability only
$\approx0.3\%$, and the alternative, an early false rejection, has probability at most a few percent
($4.5\%$ for e-LOND, $2.2\%$ for LORD++, in simulation at a stated $\nCal$). The benign-only arm of
\cref{apptab:prevalence} produces no rejection on any stream, which with five effective windows is
consistent with that rate rather than a test of it ($\Pr(\text{zero}\mid4.5\%)=0.79$). Deleting thinned
hypotheses instead of replacing them shortens $T$ and raises the margin by up to $+0.013$, yet gives the
same collapse to within $0.02$ in bootstrap probability, so it is prevalence and not re-indexing that does
the work.

\emph{Horizon misspecification (single cell).} Online e-BH's advantage over the covered procedures is
robustness to a misspecified horizon. At the stress window and one seed, the only cell we sweep the
horizon on, online e-BH keeps 144 of its 152 rejections under a $100\times$ over-estimate of $T$, where
LOND keeps all 151 at $2\times$ but none at $100\times$, and LORD++ none already at $2\times$; read this as
an illustration of the mechanism, not a measured rate.

\emph{Closure and donation, measured.} Runs here do pass the $1/\delta$ rejection count at which
\cref{prop:donation} stops applying (30, 31 and 73 rejections at 0.70, 0.77 and 0.85, where the realised
boost reaches $1.83$ against the cold-start ceiling $1.05$); what holds is the weaker fact that the
denominator never vanishes on these streams. Both procedures buy $+0$ true detections under the canonical
order across the ten cells and $+1$ and $+0$ under first-flow arrival (\cref{apptab:uai26}).

\subsection{Within-bucket order}\label{sec:transfer}

Ordering is robust to timestamp ties (at most 1.3\% of episodes share a first timestamp; all 36 reported
statistics are invariant across 50 randomised within-timestamp orders). The within-bucket order, by
contrast, is load-bearing, and it is where \cref{thm:family1} becomes visible: only a prefix of
$902/902/865/824/748$ steps can reject at all, so a detection needs a near-ceiling episode to land inside
it, which under first-flow arrival is exactly what live-fire attack traffic does. Sequencing the same
episodes by their own metadata takes the counts from $18/13/30/31/72$ to $3/11/0/0/34$
(\cref{apptab:ordering}). Over 50 pre-committed orders the count has median 1 at the primary window and
23.5 at the stress window; the canonical order is not a uniformly typical draw (above the ensemble median
at three windows, at its maximum at 0.62, below it at the two where nothing detects), while first-flow
matches or exceeds the ensemble maximum at every window and seed.

\emph{Why that canonical order.} First-flow arrival is an attacker timing lever, and the damage reaches
beyond the attacker's own hypothesis: one pad at the start of its bucket moves 373 other episodes at the primary
window, shifting the $\gamma_j$ and hence the $\mathcal M_j$ each is offered. A tie-break reading only the
group's own key makes that 0 by construction, so the \cref{assump:groupval}-preservation argument of
\cref{app:proofs} describes the order the paper reports. Three qualifications are measured rather than
assumed. It removes the timing lever, not the metadata one: appending flows moves no other group, but an
adversary that \emph{creates} keys moves ranks. A public hash is grindable over the attacker-selectable
$(\mathrm{SrcIP},\mathrm{DstIP})$: with the feasible prefix at $1.6$--$2.6\%$, an adversary trying 100
candidate keys lands one inside it with probability $0.80$--$0.93$, so the canonical order is a
canonicalisation, not a security mechanism. A keyed hash removes grinding at the price of a seed the
adversary can neither learn nor infer: blind insertion still suppresses every one of the 84 targets in
every draw at a budget of $10^{6}$ keys, at $2.1$--$89\times$ (median $19\times$) the instantiated-key
cost, with a per-window median $N_{99}$ of up to $3.2\times10^{4}$ endpoint pairs
(\cref{apptab:blindkey}). That cost is a
capability, not a computation: on a network where only a few hundred reachable pairs are available the
seed is a hard stop, a regime these figures do not cover. The measurement assumes the target sits in the
first bucket of its window, which holds at every cell here; where an earlier bucket exists the adversary
inserts there and precedes the target with probability one. One keyed instance detects 0 at the primary
window.

\subsection{Windows, seeds and orders}\label{app:padwindows}

\subsection{Sizing without the oracle}\label{app:nonoracle}

Multiplying the attacker's own episode by $c$ defeats an alert whenever $c\ge1+r^{\star}_t/m_t$. That
overshoot factor has median $1.57$ at the canonical primary window and maximum $6.17$ at the stress
window, and it is what the attacker must cover --- never the threshold itself. Tuning $c$ per window is
cheaper than the fixed $c=10$ the body reports ($c=3$ suppresses all three primary alerts at a median of
80 added flows, $3.5\times$), but that choice reads the realised costs and is reported as an
oracle-informed floor, not an attacker rule; so is the flat pad, where 33 flows suffice at the primary
window and 17 for $90\%$ of the secondary window's eleven. The one exception is the first-flow stress
arm, where overshoot reaches $4{,}302$ and no multiplier we test suffices (\cref{apptab:nonoracle}).
The fixed $c=10$ the body reports costs a median $15.7\times$ the oracle at the primary window and
$5.1\times$ at the stress window, so dropping to \statefree\ sizing costs a factor of about ten, not the
$10^{4}$--$10^{6}$ span of $1/\alphat$ within a window. The resulting pad is large but not anomalous:
the padded primary-window episode, arity $120$, is exceeded by $0.9$--$9.9\%$ of deployment episodes.

\subsection{Calibration validity and the labels}\label{sec:tail}

The measured-to-nominal benign firing ratio stays near one across the ten position$\times$seed
configurations (median $1.94\times$; independently $0.86$--$2.03\times$ on the AIT \texttt{wilson}
testbed), with a single exception, the stress window 0.85, where the benign firing rate runs $50.9\times$
nominal (exact interval $[37.3,67.9]$), so $\mathbb E[e]\approx51$ and the evidence is not a valid e-value
there (\cref{apptab:tail}). The excess is sharply localised: three host pairs carry 44 of the 46 firing
benign flows, and removing them restores the ratio to $2.22\times$. It is best explained by label error,
which distribution shift, a new benign mode, duplicated records and Mondrian conformal each fail to
account for, though we do not prove it. The other four windows are \emph{not refuted} ($1.07$--$3.79\times$,
every interval containing 1, and at 0.55 the interval $[0.03,5.96]$ excludes essentially nothing); that
is a failure to reject and not support.

The marginal rate is the weakest possible evidence for \cref{assump:groupval}, so we interrogate it
conditionally, stratifying the firing benign flows on the observable components of $\mathcal M_j$ (arity,
service, bucket, destination host) at rank depths $k\in\{1,\ldots,1000\}$, resampling both the shared
calibration tail and the test side (\cref{apptab:a1strata}). At the depth the pipeline uses the data
mostly cannot speak: 129 of 134 strata carry no test at $k=1$ (428 of 536 cells across the four depths),
and none that are estimable reject at the primary or secondary window. Deeper in the tail, 32 contrasts
survive correction at all five windows (25 after removing the one confounder we can identify), the
strongest an arity bin at 0.77. A departure at $k=1000$ is compatible with a valid rank-1 rate, so the
guarantee-bearing claims stay stated under \cref{assump:groupval}, not supported by this diagnostic. The
Bates calibration-conditional adjustment (\cref{apptab:bates}) is stronger than e-LOND needs and is
treated as optional.

\subsection{Calibrating the group directly}\label{sec:groupcal}

The constructive alternative to \cref{assump:groupval} is to run split conformal on the grouped unit,
same key and protocol, so that only calibration/test \emph{group} exchangeability is required. Built and
measured over two group statistics, five windows and two seeds (\cref{apptab:groupcal}), it discharges
the premise but fails as a repair. Group exchangeability is still a deployment assumption, and our threat
model is one under which test groups are partly adversary-constituted: insertion instantiates endpoint
pairs that enter the stream as true nulls, so the group-formation process is something the adversary
moves. The \textsc{max} statistic makes that fragility acute: its rank correlation with arity is
$+0.23$--$+0.41$ across the ten cells, and while marginal coverage is roughly right ($0.55$--$2.32\times$
nominal), arity-conditional coverage runs from $0.13\times$ to $24.2\times$ nominal under \textsc{max}
against $0.00$--$5.6\times$ under the mean. Stratifying calibration on arity restores a per-stratum
guarantee and leaves each stratum a cold-start window of $2$--$52$ hypotheses. The robustness and the
fragility are one fact: appending cannot lower a maximum, which is why \cref{thm:padding} does not run
against it and why the arity dependence is there at all.

Grant the repair its premise and it still cannot alert. Group calibration replaces $1.8$--$2.4$M flows by
$37$--$57$k groups, so $\ceil$ falls $40$--$49\times$ at unchanged $T$: the margin moves from $+0.067$ to
$-0.977$ at the primary window, no window is feasible, and detection at the primary and secondary
windows falls from 18 and 13 (first-flow) to zero. At 0.55 the cold-start window is 81 hypotheses while
the first of 110 firing groups arrives at position 401, and feasible-step counts match
$\lfloor(\alpha\ceil(R{+}1)/\zeta(1.6))^{1/1.6}\rfloor$ everywhere. Calibration groups containing any
malicious flow are excluded, dropping up to $26.1\%$ of calibration flows in groups whose mean arity runs
to $45.4\times$ the benign mean, a bias that favours firing, so the negative result survives its own bias.

\subsection{Calibration contamination}\label{sec:contamination}

At $k=1$ the conformal rule is one order statistic, the calibration maximum, so contamination acts only
through it. Injecting $j$ attack flows above that maximum hands the threshold to the attacker once
$j\ge k$, so tolerable adversarial mislabels are exactly $k-1=0$. One mislabelled flow above the
calibration maximum takes episode recall to $0.000$, from $0.011$ at 0.55 and $0.133$ at 0.85 under the
canonical order ($0.065$ and $0.282$ under first-flow arrival). A random mislabel is milder but not much:
median recall $0.004$ at 0.55 and $0.035$ at 0.85, with zero recall in $22.5\%$ and $32.25\%$ of draws,
at a scale $\varepsilon^{*}=1/(Nq_0)$ of $1.11$--$1.31$ flows. A contaminating flow \emph{below} the
maximum only inflates the p-value denominator, degrading the guarantee to at most $q(1+\varepsilon)$, so
power dies at one flow while validity barely moves. The feasibility margin is not a safety indicator: it
stays at $+0.436$ throughout the sweep that destroys detection.

\section{Adaptive Controller-State Extension}\label{app:state}

\emph{Question answered.} A controller can escape the finite horizon by conditioning its spending
index on observed evidence (\cref{app:taxonomy}). Does that escape create a new attack surface?
\emph{Takeaway.} It does: the attacker supplies the evidence that drives the state. The result is
mechanism-specific and conspicuous in volume, which is why it is an extension rather than one of the
paper's main empirical claims.

The padding attack of \cref{sec:attack} manipulates the composition of one hypothesis. A distinct surface appears when a
controller escapes \cref{thm:family1} by conditioning its spending index on observed evidence. ADDIS
advances its index on selected non-candidates, whose p-values lie in $(\lambda,\tau]$, and never rejects
them. Concretely it offers $\alphat=\min\{\lambda,\,(\tau-\lambda)[W\gamma_{S^t-C_{0+}}+\cdots]\}$ at
$\lambda=0.25$, $\tau=0.5$, where $S^t-C_{0+}$ counts exactly those selected non-candidates and $\gamma$
is the $0$-indexed $(j+1)^{-1.6}/\zeta(1.6)$; the attack moves that index and nothing else
(\cref{app:procedures}). An attacker who prepends groups in the interval therefore advances the index
without spending any discovery of its own, and can force the future level below the conformal floor.

\begin{theorem}[ADDIS state budget]\label{thm:addis}
Under ADDIS's $0$-indexed $\gamma_j=(j+1)^{-1.6}/\zeta(1.6)$ with $0<\lambda<\tau<1$, rank-$k$
conformal evidence, precursors injected at index $0$, and a cap at or above the conformal floor,
$\lambda\ge k/(\nCal+1)$, the number of precursor hypotheses that silences every later target is
$B^{\star}$, the smallest $D$ with
\[
D+1>\Big[\frac{(\tau-\lambda)\,W\,(\nCal+1)}{k\,\zeta(1.6)}\Big]^{1/1.6},
\]
where $W=w_0$ before the first rejection. After $R\ge1$ rejections ADDIS's reward terms sit at their own
selected-non-candidate lags instead of collapsing to one index. Their coefficients still sum to exactly
$\alpha R$, but each lagged term carries $\gamma_{D-\text{lag}}\ge\gamma_D$ because $\gamma$ decreases, so
the driving level is at least $(\tau-\lambda)\alpha R\gamma_D$ and feasibility survives \emph{longer}.
The displayed expression evaluated at $W=\alpha R$ is therefore a \emph{lower} bound on $B^{\star}$, not
an upper one --- with a lag of $100$ at $R=1$ it returns $313$ where $B^{\star}=373$. The exact case is
$R=0$, which is the state the attack is mounted in, since the precursors precede every target. The cap hypothesis is what makes
the driving term rather than the cap binding: if $\lambda<k/(\nCal+1)$ then $\alphat\le\lambda$ is
already below the floor and no target can be rejected at any $D$, so the budget is trivially zero and
the displayed expression overstates it. Our configuration satisfies it by six orders of magnitude
($\lambda\ceil=4.5\times10^{5}$). Each precursor can be a group
with one firing flow and enough zero-evidence flows to place its p-value in $(\lambda,\tau]$; the smallest
such group has $m^{\star}=\lfloor\lambda(\nCal+1)/k\rfloor+1$ flows, which is a valid precursor whenever
$m^{\star}\le\tau\ceil$ --- guaranteed by $(\tau-\lambda)\ceil\ge1$, and satisfied by six orders of
magnitude here. The total injected volume is $B^{\star}m^{\star}$.
\end{theorem}

At the stress window (position 0.85, first-flow order) the closed form is verified against the
implementation on the real stream: $202$ precursors leave all 152 ADDIS rejections intact and $203$
eliminate all of them, permanently (\cref{fig:addis}B, \cref{apptab:addisstate}). Because
that window's evidence is not a valid e-value, and the two-point structure that lets ADDIS escape is the
same property that violates its conservativeness condition, this demonstrates the mechanism and prices
it; it is not an FDR-valid real-data result. The attack survives a grey-box setting: the admissible group
size is a $\tau/\lambda$-wide interval, so a $\pm33\%$ estimate of $\nCal$ suffices and unknown controller
state costs about $36\times$ over-provisioning.

To isolate the mechanism from that validity failure we generate streams with genuinely conservative
independent $\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$ null p-values and 120 strong targets. ADDIS's empirical all-null $\FDR$
is $0.010$ and its mixed-stream mean $\FDP$ is $0.047$ at recall $1.00$, both below $q=0.05$. We then
prepend \emph{alternative}, non-null precursors in $(\lambda,\tau]$, so the assumptions on true nulls are
untouched and the guarantee holds on the attacked stream. The attack silences all 120 targets at a median
$B^{\star}=140$ over 300 streams (range $140$--$255$; \cref{fig:addis}C, \cref{apptab:addissynth}). The
guarantee therefore remains valid while the security objective is defeated: controlling the fraction of
false discoveries does not require making any discovery.

The real-stream cost is conspicuous, not cheap. At $\nCal=1{,}813{,}113$ each precursor needs $453{,}279$
flows and 203 precursors need $92{,}015{,}637$, about 36.1~GB or 33.4~Mbit/s over the deployment span
and $37.5\times$ the window's own flow count; the total scales as
$\lambda\nCal\,[(\tau-\lambda)w_0\nCal]^{0.625}/k^{1.625}$, so the calibration budget that buys
feasibility also buys resistance. Suppressing one canonical e-LOND alert at the same window costs a
median of 116 flows. The result is also mechanism-specific: online e-BH decides by a fixed point
over the whole history rather than by a spending index, so this attack has no state variable to
move. We do not claim that online e-BH is immune to every manipulation, only that \cref{thm:addis} does
not cover it. Where both surfaces apply to ADDIS they coincide: when its level cap $\lambda$ binds, the minimal
suppressing pad also lands in $(\lambda,\tau]$ and advances the index (101 of ADDIS's 147 true
detections; \cref{app:proofs}). Against ADDIS the state attack is also the cheaper one: its level is
five orders of magnitude above e-LOND's, so padding one episode costs a median $3.4\times10^{7}$ flows
and state manipulation wins from the third episode on.

\subsection{Analyst-feedback controllers}

The three feedback baselines of \cref{apptab:feedback} are threshold controllers, not $\FDR$ procedures.
Each carries a single state $u_t\in[0,1]$, a position in the calibration score distribution: it alerts on
episode $t$ when that episode's maximum flow score exceeds the $u_t$-quantile of the calibration scores,
and moves $u_t$ only when an analyst's disposition of an earlier alert arrives, after a wall-clock delay
$L$. Writing $\widehat{\FDP}_t$ for the false fraction over the last 200 dispositions received so far and
$\varepsilon_t=\widehat{\FDP}_t-q$,
{\small\begin{align*}
\textbf{P:}\ &u_{t+1}=u_t+\eta_P\,\varepsilon_t,\\
\textbf{PI:}\ &u_{t+1}=u_t+\eta_P\,\varepsilon_t+\eta_I\textstyle\sum_{r\le t}\varepsilon_r,\\
\textbf{AQT:}\ &u_{t+1}=u_t+\eta\big[(1{-}q)\mathbf 1\{\text{false}\}-q\mathbf 1\{\text{true}\}\big],
\end{align*}}%
with the PI integral clamped against windup and $u$ clipped to $[0,1]$. Gains are chosen per window,
delay and controller by grid search ($\eta_P\in\{0.005,0.02,0.05,0.2\}$, window $\in\{50,200\}$,
$u_0\in\{0.99,0.999\}$, AQT $\eta\in\{10^{-4},10^{-3},5\times10^{-3},0.02\}$, $\eta_I=\eta_P/10$)
minimising $(\lvert\FDP-q\rvert,-\text{recall})$ on the realised test labels, an ex-post oracle, so the
reported performance is an upper bound, the conservative direction for the negative conclusion. Under
first-flow order they reach $\FDP\,0.402$ (P, PI) and $0.523$ (AQT) at fifteen-minute disposition delay,
agree with one another by four hours, and reach the no-feedback threshold ($0.841$) only by eight: a
statement about delay on these controllers, not about feedback-aware testing~\cite{lu2026gaif}, whose
input our measurement prices.

\subsection{Negative results}

\emph{Inflating a benign group cannot create a discovery at a fixed level.} The argument is pathwise,
not distributional: for the arithmetic mean with evidence sum $S$ over $m$ members, appending $r$
zero-evidence flows gives $S/(m+r)\le S/m$, so a group that is not rejected at a fixed level with its
existing evidence is not rejected after padding either. The bound $\mathbb E[\Ev(G)\mid\mathcal M_j]\le1$
of \cref{assump:groupval} does not by itself give this: an expectation bound permits large individual
realisations (an e-value equal to $100$ with probability $0.01$ and zero otherwise has mean one and
crosses a threshold of $20$), which is exactly why a benign group can be rejected and why the
$\FDR$ guarantee is a rate, not an exclusion. \emph{Fragmentation leaves expected group evidence
unchanged, and no more.} For a pure-attack group whose $m$ flows fire independently with probability
$\phi$, $\mathbb E[\Ev(G)]=\phi\ceil$ does not depend on $m$; the rejection probability does,
$\Pr(\Ev(G)\ge\theta)=\Pr(\mathrm{Binomial}(m,\phi)\ge\lceil m\theta/\ceil\rceil)$, and splitting a
campaign also changes the number of hypotheses and the controller state, so we make no analytic claim
about its effect on detection. \emph{Boosting recovers nothing for two-point evidence:} the
optimal e-to-e boosting factor for a $\{0,\ceil\}$-valued e-value is $b^{\star}=1$ by construction.
\emph{Raising $k$} removes the operating point rather than trading power for validity; \emph{randomised
smoothing} removes the floor but not the reliably-detected set (\cref{app:taxonomy}); \emph{asymmetric
weighting} is not a free mitigation (\cref{sec:fixes}); \emph{stratified (Mondrian) conformal} does not
repair the position-0.85 anomaly; and the published $\alpha$-death fix (mem-e-LORD) is identical to e-LORD
before the first rejection.
